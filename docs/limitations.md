# Limitations

What the prototype does not establish, in one place. Each item says how large the problem is, what evidence we have, and what would fix it. Items are ordered by how much they matter for the claims made in `README.md` and `PITCH_BAPAT.md`.

## 1. What the false alarm guarantee covers

* **The null is a statement about conditional means.** Theorems A and B (`docs/math.md`, 5.4) hold when the conditional mean of the truncated squared return stays at (or below, for the increase side) its baseline. Under persistent volatility clustering this is false even when the unconditional variance never changes, and the monitor then alarms on most days (E9, Q9c: 12% to 20% of days even with a band of 1 when the GARCH half-life is an hour or more). These alarms are real changes in conditional volatility, but a user who only cares about structural breaks will count them as false. Remedy: monitor returns divided by a predictable volatility forecast (E10), which restores validity when the forecast model is right (a proof also needs the true or the confidence-sequence baseline), at the cost of power against breaks the forecast absorbs.
* **How fast the bound degrades is known only in sup or pathwise form (TH4).** If the conditional mean drifts above its baseline by at most δ, the bound is m/A times a closed-form factor (`docs/theory_robustness.md`, Theorem 1); it is attained only where it reaches 1, and below that the strongest stream found reaches 0.33 to 0.63 of it at one day. A drift that varies needs a pathwise bound (Theorems 2 and 3), proved for an exogenous drift or in a looser threshold form; the mean drift alone gives no bound of the form C m / A. For GARCH clustering the proved bound is below 1 in only 22 of 64 cells at one day, all with a small drift, so where the nominal bound fails the theory says little.
* **The baseline is estimated.** With a plug-in baseline the bound holds only approximately (E4d; E10a found about 5% within the first day after a one-day calibration window against a bound that is about 1% with the true baseline). The excess grows with the horizon: 44% against 25% over five days (E10, Q10b2) and 51% to 57% against 35% over seven days at band 0 (ALT5, Q1). With only 500 calibration steps, its offline size in E13 was 11% to 19% instead of a nominal 5%. Over 30-day streams the plug-in raises 0.084 to 0.094 false alarms per day against a nominal 0.05 (E14), and no bound of the form C m / A can hold for it at a fixed calibration length (Proposition A.6 and Lemma A.7 in `docs/theory_running_tau.md`). The confidence-sequence baseline (E8, `docs/math.md` section 6) makes the bound `delta + m / A` hold with an estimated baseline, under the stated assumptions, and the rank monitor of E14 needs no baseline. TH5 (`docs/theory_limits.md`, Theorem 8 and Corollary 10) locates the boundary for the worst stream: m / A holds up to a factor 1 + O(m / √n) when the horizon m is small compared with √n and can fail by an unbounded factor when m is large compared with √n and log A, so a one-day horizon after a one-day window (√390 ≈ 20) is already past it. The distribution-free constant (1.79) makes that bound informative only for m below about √n / 20, and for i.i.d. monitored returns the boundary is a heuristic compared with simulations after the fact, not a theorem.
* **Recalibration after an alarm.** Each monitored segment has its own guarantee. Theorem B itself bounds only the first day after each (re)calibration: on later days of a segment the chance of a false alarm can exceed 5% (TH6: 4.7% on average over days 11 to 20 for i.i.d. returns, 5.25% for a null stream that watches the detector, 28% on a day after nine calm days); only the long-run average of 0.05 per day (Corollary C) holds. Restarting the statistics every day gives a proved 5% for every day only with a known baseline (`docs/theory_per_period.md`); with the confidence-sequence baseline it gives δ + m / A for each day of a segment, not given the past and only while Theorem D's tolerance holds to the end of that day (which the GARCH models of the next bullet do not meet with any small band), and with the plug-in baseline nothing per day is proved. Statements about "alarms per day" over a long series are averages over segments, not a single anytime-valid statement for the whole series (Corollary C bounds the expected number of false alarms). With the default protocol, a day of recalibration after every alarm leaves the monitor blind about 70% of the time on crypto data (E12), and in the landmark case studies of E18 it was recalibrating at all three crypto shocks, after alarms raised 4.5 to 6.9 hours earlier as each episode began. E14's awake monitors remove the blind period with a proof, but the rank version raises 3.6 to 4.3 alarms per crypto day without a null band, and at the releases where they were awake they flagged a smaller share than the plug-in did when it was awake (not yet explained).
* **The confidence-sequence baseline can be blind, and real calm days are not a null (E19).** With the confidence-sequence baseline the increase side cannot alarm at all when τ² is at most the upper confidence limit of the baseline: on the fresh BTC data of 2026 it happened for 28% of the monitored time, one segment lasting 47 days (E23), and round 4 found the same mode on Bitstamp BTC/USD at the same time (29 days from 16 August 2026, against 49 days on Binance BTC in R4's continuous run), so it is not specific to one venue (`reports/round4/R4/REPORT.md`); in E19's runs it happened in 5 of 7031 segments on the five coins, all on BTC, once for 136 days (found after the run; E24 proves and implements a guard, `avmonitor.guard`, which removed every blind segment on BTC, at the cost of one more calibration day where a window is discarded; it is not the default, and its real-data results are post hoc, because it needs its own proof). The checks on real data that pass use permuted calm days, which are exchangeable by construction; calm days resampled in blocks make every monitor, the exact rank detector included, alarm within a day on 86% to 100% of streams, so on real minute data the null itself rarely holds and real alarm rates are not false alarm rates. Parts of E19 were not run on Nifty.
* **The confidence-sequence baseline needs a stable conditional mean (TH6).** Theorem D (`docs/math.md` 6.4, restated) needs the conditional mean over the next m steps to stay within a band b of the calibration window's values. Under GARCH(1,1) clustering the band needed had a median of 0.90 to 5.01, and no simulated path met b = 0.25; the version with no assumption adds the probability that the band fails, which is then large. The e-CUSUM's bound for every window of a day holds only for exchangeable nulls (a null stream that parks the statistic below the threshold and then strikes breaks it, Proposition 3 of `docs/theory_per_period.md`).
* **Several assets.** Running the monitor on five coins with a 5% budget each gives, by the union bound, up to about 25% chance of at least one false alarm across the group in the first day after calibration, and up to 0.25 expected false alarms per day across the group in the long run. E15 (`docs/theory_panel.md`) gives a panel monitor with a proved false discovery rate at every stopping time, using e-BH on per-asset e-processes. Two limits remain: the theory does not control the chance of at least one false discovery once real changes are present (8% in one design), and over ten daily blocks with changes that chance reached 23.5% against 4.25% for a Bonferroni split. On real data e-BH mostly gains timing over Bonferroni (a median 2 to 4 minutes earlier), not many more discoveries.
* **Several sampling frequencies (E17).** The certified average of 1, 5 and 15 minute detectors is proved only when each frequency's own null holds, which follows from the one-minute null for independent returns, a scale fixed within blocks or raw squares under martingale differences, but not in general (`docs/theory_multifreq.md`, section 2.1). Under price noise the one-minute detector is invalid and only an average of 5 and 15 minute detectors on gap blocks keeps the proof. The plain average checked every minute behaved like the certified one in every simulation, but its proved bound is three times larger (Theorem 3, balanced weights). All E17 bounds assume the true baselines; with the plug-in baselines the one-minute detector and the equal-weight average exceeded 5% per day in simulation.
* **The bound is conservative.** At one day it is about 4 times larger than the observed rate with the true baseline (E9), and about 2 times larger than the worst rate an adversarial search found inside the theorem's conditions (E16: 2.62% within the first day; 1.4 times over five days). A search only gives lower bounds on the worst case; what is proved is only 0.98 of the bound at one day, and a certified sharper bound for the default grid would need an exact computation we did not attempt. This costs detection speed; a sharper threshold is studied in E11 but the default keeps the proven level. For the README setting (plug-in baseline, null band 0.25) the threshold is far safer still: it raised 0 false alarms in 20,000 simulated calm days, and the same statistic at a threshold that uses the 5% budget detects 3.1 to 6.4 times faster; at an equal measured false-alarm rate the e-detector statistic is about as fast as the classical rules on Gaussian returns and much faster on heavy tails (round 4, `reports/round4/R2/REPORT.md`) [SYNTHETIC].

* **The monitor watches the clipped squared return, not the variance (round 4, R3) [SYNTHETIC].** A change in the shape of the tails at constant variance moves the mean of the clipped square, so the monitor treats it as a change: when Student t returns switch from 30 to 3 degrees of freedom at unit variance, the known-baseline detector's decrease side fires within a day on 65% of paths (the README setting's band hides most of this, 1.0%), and the reverse switch makes the README setting fire on 45% within a day, all on the increase side. The mirror case also holds: a doubling or tripling of variance carried only by rare 20 sd jumps moves the clipped mean by 2% to 4%, and the README setting never alarmed within five days. So "volatility change" in the README means a change in the clipped square at the chosen truncation level (`reports/round4/R3/REPORT.md`).
* **Removing the intraday pattern and bid-ask bounce (round 4, R3) [SYNTHETIC].** With a U-shaped intraday pattern estimated from the past 20 days (`data.intraday_scale`, `start="past"`) and divided out, as the real-data experiments do, the known-baseline detector stays within its one-day bound (3.4%) but reaches 32.2% over five days against 25% (37.3% for a stronger pattern), and the plug-in baseline without a band 35.5%; the README setting (0.1%) and the confidence-sequence baseline (0.2%) stay far inside. This is the measurement that `docs/math.md` 5.6.3 lists as missing. Strong bid-ask bounce (MA(1) returns with theta = -0.6) breaks the known-baseline five-day bound (40.7% against 25%) and the band-0 plug-in's one-day bound (8.6%); theta = -0.3, skewed returns, missing values, tick grids with up to 60% zero returns and GARCH(1,1) forecasts fitted to GJR, EGARCH-type or stochastic-volatility data all stayed within the proved bounds.
* **A change during calibration, a short burst (round 4, R3) [SYNTHETIC].** If volatility doubles halfway through the calibration window, the baseline is learnt about 26% too low and the README setting stays silent for five days on 56% of paths (the confidence-sequence baseline does better). A 30-minute burst of three times the variance is caught inside it or within the hour after on only 27% of paths (a 60-minute burst: 82%). A gradual doubling over one day is caught on every path within a day after the ramp ends.

## 2. Real-data validation has no ground truth

* Whether a volatility regime "really" changed at a given minute is not observable. The real-data checks (E5, E6, E7, E10) are indirect: realised volatility after the alarm, comparison with forecasts, India VIX, and scheduled events. Volatility clusters, so any detector that reacts to a burst will look somewhat predictive; the random-time and forecast controls reduce but do not remove this.
* Data come from public mirrors (Binance klines via a third-party GitHub archive, a cleaned Nifty 50 archive, a Bitstamp archive), not from the exchanges directly. The loaders report gaps, duplicates and bad prices, and `data/MANIFEST.csv` pins the exact bytes used. The Bitstamp results (E5, the Bitstamp rows of E6 and E19, E7 Q7a, E8 Q8e) were computed when the loader turned the file's filled, zero-volume minutes (0.30%) into zero returns, against `docs/math.md` 5.6; the scripts keep that setting so that they reproduce the committed numbers, and `results/r9_bitstamp_impact.csv` gives the effect of the fix (no conclusion changes; review round 9, m5).
* The paper does not name its crypto venue, so our reproductions of Tables 5 and 6 compare against a likely different data set (E6 discusses which features match).
* **Real-data inference (E20).** Only one real-data test was pre-specified as primary (FOMC and CPI on BTC; CPI did not hold). E22 reran it under a pre-registration with the confidence-sequence baseline restarted every day: FOMC holds (Holm p = 0.00033) and CPI passes too (Holm 0.0097), but the CPI result depends on the configuration (p = 0.12 without the restart), and the data are not new (E12's result was known when E22 was designed). That monitor alarmed on about half of its monitored days, so the ±25% tolerance of its bound fails on real days and E22 does not test the bound. **The confirmatory test on fresh data failed (E23):** on the FOMC statements and CPI releases of January to October 2026, pre-registered before any 2026 crypto return was loaded, the same monitor flagged 2 of 16 releases on BTC against 5.9% of matched controls (p = 0.25, planned power 0.82). The FOMC contrast of 2024 and 2025 should therefore be read as exploratory. Only the never-blind rank monitor's FOMC cells held (secondary, 4 of 6 on each coin, Holm 0.016 to 0.055), with six statements. E23 also found that E12's stratified test can exceed its nominal level slightly when releases share control windows (5.8% for seven FOMC statements in the planning simulation). Two forward tests are pre-registered on releases nobody has seen: the fixed-date test of E24 (1 June 2027) and the anytime-valid e-process of E25, which needs no independence between strata but rests on a working null (each release window exchangeable with its own controls given the earlier strata) that no property of the monitor implies, and which can fail in two ways the design does not remove: most strata have controls only after the release (a falling alarm rate then favours rejection, though a rate halving every 91 days raised the simulated false rejection rate only from 2.3% to 2.6%, review round 11), and four pairs of releases fall on consecutive days, so the monitor's state after one release carries into the next release's window but not into its controls. E25 is less powerful than E24's test at E24's date (0.43 against 0.82 at the planning rates), and if either is used to call the contrast confirmed, two 5% tests were run on the same releases (at most 10% together). Every other real-data result is secondary or exploratory, and pooled two-proportion tests in E6, E13 and E17 treat dependent alarms as independent; the day-block bootstrap of E20 gives intervals 1.2 to 1.5 times wider. Against a forecast with an intraday component the alarms add little information about next-day realised variance.
* **Comparisons with published methods (E13, E21).** The two-window variance-ratio scan is our construction inspired by Bibinger, Jirak and Vetter (2017), not their procedure: the full text could not be opened from our environment (conclusions use simulation-calibrated critical values). E21 added a Student t BOCPD and CUSUM monitoring rules of the Horváth-Hušková-Kokoszka-Steinebach type; the exact published procedures and a β-divergence BOCPD were not implemented for the same reason. E21's real-data p-values are unadjusted (21 of 64 below 0.05) and its count matching needed a grid search after a timing run. Classical rules on the same truncated statistic matched the e-detector on real data, so the evidence does not show that the e-detector is more precise than a well-calibrated classical rule on the same statistic, only that it needs no calibration. The real-data comparison matches alarm counts on the same data, treats alarms as independent in its p-values (so they are optimistic), and only the January to June 2025 crypto half-year is fully held out for the e-detector.

* **What a real alarm shows (round 4, R4 and V1) [REAL].** Run continuously on every asset and year from 2017 to October 2026, up alarms are followed by an hour above the calibrated level in 81% to 98% of cases per asset and year, against 25% to 38% at random watched minutes, and this held in 2026 too. But random minutes whose last hour was just as volatile are followed by a high hour almost as often (79% against 93% for BTC in 2025), and the hour after an up alarm is calmer than the hour before it in 61% to 68% of cases, partly by construction (an alarm fires right after a run of large returns). So an alarm says that volatility is now well above its calibrated level, not that it will keep rising. The blind share after alarms is 64% to 72% on crypto in every year, but 43% to 65% on the Nifty 50 (`reports/round4/R4/REPORT.md`, `reports/round4/V1/REPORT.md`).
* **Data vendor changes (round 4, R1) [REAL].** The 2026-08 to 2026-10 Nifty files freeze the last 14 minutes of every session (97% of the bars from 15:15 to 15:28 IST are flat) and put the whole closing move in the 15:29 bar; E7's frozen-run rule drops these minutes, so the live pre-registered Nifty run monitors about 360 returns per session, not the 375 its configuration states. The Binance 2026-10 files on the mirror end on 24 October 2026, which is why E23 has 16 of the 17 releases of January to October 2026 (the 28 October FOMC statement is listed in `results/e23_events_excluded.csv`). All 139 fixed data files match `data/MANIFEST.csv` byte for byte, and the first 956,327 rows of the daily Bitstamp file still match its committed hash (`reports/round4/R1/REPORT.md`).

## 3. Estimation

* Truncation does not remove microstructure noise (`docs/estimator_issues.md`, 4b). At one-minute sampling the noise is small for the assets used here; at higher frequencies a noise-robust first stage would be needed (alternative `alternatives/preaveraging/`).
* Clipping keeps τ⁴ for each large jump, so very frequent large jumps still bias truncated quarticity upward (E2c). Dropping instead of clipping (threshold RQ) removes more of this.
* The κ₄ bias correction is exact only for locally Gaussian diffusive returns.
* A fixed-accuracy estimate of the volatility level (alternative A8) is, under volatility clustering, an estimate of the recent window's average conditional variance, not of a long-run level; reaching the long-run level took 2,000 to 19,000 one-minute returns in simulation. Its confidence sequences are valid for that window average only without the running intersection.
* Our reading of how the paper's tables were computed (unnormalised weights, raw MAD, no factor n in RQ) is an inference from the published numbers. It needs the author's code to confirm, and is presented as questions, not corrections.

## 4. Scope

* **Delay limits (TH5).** The lower bound on the mean delay of every valid deterministic procedure is for a change at the first monitored step, is estimated by Monte Carlo, and may not be attained, so the e-detectors' ratios to it (1.7 to 2.2) are upper bounds on their inefficiency; a floor after a pre-change history, and the delay cost of the plug-in margin z and of the confidence-sequence baseline, are not derived.
* One-minute bars only. Tick data, other venues and other asset classes are untested.
* The risk switch and correlation monitor are minimal prototypes, kept in `alternatives/`, and their results are not claims about trading performance.

## 5. Who has checked what

* **No human expert has reviewed the proofs or the code.** The eleven review rounds and three referee reports in `docs/review/` were written by separate AI agents (Claude) instructed to act as sceptical referees. They found real errors (several were fixed and are logged), but an AI reviewer can share the blind spots of the AI that wrote the files. A human check of the proofs in `docs/math.md` and the `docs/theory_*.md` notes is still needed before any claim is submitted for publication.
* **Test suite.** 762 tests (581 from the build rounds, 56 added by the 3 November validation audit, 19 by the audit fixes of PR #4 and 106 in round 4: 50 for the repairs and 56 property tests), all passing, run with `python -m pytest -q` on 4 November 2026 (OUR CHECK; no data needed). Passing tests show that the code does what the tests say, not that the theory is right.
* **Number tracing.** `scripts/trace_numbers.py` checks that each number printed in the paper draft, the README and the pitch appears in a committed Markdown report or data file. It cannot tell a right match from a coincidence: a count such as 581 can match an unrelated number elsewhere, so counts and settings are listed here by hand.
