## E21. Benchmarks that are not calibrated on Gaussian data only

Script `experiments/e21_benchmarks.py`; methods `src/avmonitor/benchmarks_robust.py`; outputs `results/e21_*.csv` and `results/figures/e21_*.png`; tests `tests/test_benchmarks_robust.py`; iteration notes `docs/iterations/E21.md`. Q21a to Q21c are **[SYNTHETIC]**, Q21d is **[REAL]**, and the literature statements are marked **[PUBLISHED]** with what could and could not be checked. Every setting, seed, target and number of paths was fixed before the reported runs (iteration 1 of the notes); the changes made after the first runs are listed in "Post hoc decisions" at the end.

**Question.** The referee (M5) observed that the competitors of E4 and E13 were calibrated on Gaussian data, that BOCPD had a Gaussian observation model, that the "BJV-type" test was reconstructed without the paper (renamed "VR scan" in E13), and that no classical detector on the e-detector's own statistic and no econometric monitoring procedure was tried, so that the benchmarks favour the proposed method. Three questions follow. (i) At the same false alarm rate on clean data, what does Ville's distribution-free threshold cost in delay against a threshold calibrated for the true law? (ii) When the law is not Gaussian, does a threshold calibrated by simulation or by a bootstrap of the calibration window keep its rate? (iii) How do a robust BOCPD and a CUSUM monitoring procedure with a training sample compare, on simulated and on real data?

**Summary.**
* **At equal false alarm rate on Gaussian data, Ville's threshold costs the e-detector's statistic almost nothing.** [SYNTHETIC] With thresholds calibrated by simulation to the e-detector's own first-day rate on clean Gaussian data (1.3%), the same Shiryaev-Roberts betting statistic detects increases of the sd by 25%, 50% and 100% in 106, 41 and 20 steps against the e-detector's 100, 39 and 19, and decreases about 10% faster (144 against 162 steps at × 0.8). A Page CUSUM on the same truncated statistic is as fast for increases and 15% to 40% faster for decreases, where the e-detector's bets are capped by validity (E11). The price of a distribution-free threshold shows only against thresholds that spend the full 5% per day: 1.1 to 1.3 times the delay for increases and 1.4 to 2.1 times for decreases, at 4.5% to 4.9% false alarms per day instead of 1.3%, and only if the law is known.
* **Thresholds calibrated on Gaussian data do not carry over to other laws, even on the truncated statistic; a bootstrap of the calibration window repairs this for i.i.d. laws.** [SYNTHETIC] Gaussian-calibrated CUSUMs on X reach 5.0% to 8.2% per day under Student t3 and t5 (target 1.3%) and 14% to 22% (target 5%); the HKZ-type rule on raw r² reaches 10% to 15% under Student t3 and jumps. Per-path bootstrap thresholds stay at 1.4% to 2.2% (target 1.3%) and 4.9% to 6.3% (target 5%) under all six i.i.d.-type laws. With bootstrap thresholds under Student t3 the e-detector detects increases 16% to 24% faster than the CUSUM, and the CUSUM detects decreases 3% to 10% faster. Under GARCH and slow stochastic volatility every rule alarms on most paths, the e-detector included; the HKZ-type rules, whose long-run variance absorbs part of the clustering, alarm least (21% to 52% in the first day).
* **A Student t BOCPD fixes plain BOCPD's false alarms but not its dependence on the law, and loses small changes.** [SYNTHETIC] On E13's paths BOCPD-t (ν = 3, exact run-length recursion on a variance grid) has 0.7% false alarms per day under jumps and 3.3% under Student t5, against 73.2% and 53.2% for E13's Gaussian BOCPD, but 4.9% under Student t3 and 0.5% under Gaussian data with the same threshold; within 1500 steps it finds only 56% of 25% increases and 77% of 20% decreases (e-detector 98%), while it is faster than the e-detector for halvings of the sd (27 against 81 steps).
* **CUSUM monitoring with a training sample (HKZ-type) is robust only on the truncated statistic and is about twice as slow.** [SYNTHETIC] On X = min(r², τ²) its rate holds under every i.i.d.-type law (1.1% to 1.6% at a 1.3% target); its delays are about twice the e-detector's, because its boundary is of order c σ sqrt(m) (derived below). Asymptotic critical values for open-ended monitoring spend almost none of their 5% in the first day for γ ≤ 0.25.
* **On real data, classical rules on the e-detector's own statistic and design are as precise as the e-detector; BOCPD-t and the HKZ-type rules are not.** [REAL] Matched to the e-detector's numbers of up and down alarms on the five coins (both half-years, bands 0 and 0.25), SR betting with a calibrated threshold and the two CUSUMs on X are never significantly less precise than the e-detector in 24 comparisons of the next-day outcome (differences -5.0 to +3.0 points; one significant difference, in favour of SR betting). The HKZ-type rules are below it in 13 of 16 comparisons (6 significant), BOCPD-t in 20 of 24 (14 significant), although BOCPD-t improves on E13's Gaussian BOCPD. E13's advantage over BOCPD and the VR scan therefore appears to come from the statistic and the design (truncated squared returns against a one-day baseline, recalibrated after each alarm), not from the betting form or from Ville's threshold; what the threshold adds is a false alarm guarantee that needs no calibration and no assumed law.
* **What could not be checked.** [PUBLISHED] The texts of Knoblauch, Jewson and Damoulas (2018), Horváth, Kokoszka and Zhang (2006) and Chu, Stinchcombe and White (1996) could not be opened from this machine. BOCPD-t is therefore not the β-divergence method, and the monitoring rules are "of the type" of HKZ and CSW, with a boundary verified for monitoring in linear models and critical values from simulation.

### The competitors

All rules see the same returns: a calibration window (2000 steps in the simulations, 1440 minutes on real data) and then the monitored stream.

* **Classical rules on the e-detector's statistic.** X_t = min(r_t², τ²) with τ = 3 times the scaled MAD of the calibration window and m0 the mean of X over that window, as for the e-detector; Y_t = X_t / m0. *Page's two-sided CUSUM on Y*: G_t = max(0, G_{t-1} + Y_t - 1 - k) for increases and max(0, G_{t-1} + 1 - Y_t - k) for decreases, with the reference value of the Gaussian likelihood ratio for a change of the variance by a factor θ (k = θ log θ / (θ - 1) - 1 for θ > 1 and k = 1 - θ log θ / (θ - 1) for θ < 1; on untruncated Gaussian returns this is exactly the likelihood-ratio CUSUM): "small" for sd × 1.25 and × 0.8 (k = 0.240 and 0.207) and "medium" for × 1.5 and × 0.67 (k = 0.460 and 0.348). *SR betting*: the e-detector's own Shiryaev-Roberts betting statistics (the same six increase and five decrease bets), with a calibrated threshold in place of the level 2A that Ville's inequality gives. Each side of a two-sided rule gets half of the target rate.
* **Two calibrations.** (a) *Gaussian simulation*: 20,000 clean Gaussian paths, one 390-step day after a 2000-step calibration window; the targets are the e-detector's own first-day rate on the same paths (1.305%, "matched") and 5% (the e-detector's bound for one day, A = 7800). (b) *Bootstrap of the calibration window*, per path: 1000 replicates, each a resampled calibration window (giving m0*) and a resampled 390-step stream of X values, Y* = X* / m0*; each side's threshold is the 1 - α/2 quantile of the replicate maxima. τ is kept (X is already truncated at τ²). This is the calibration a practitioner could run without assuming a law; it assumes i.i.d. returns.
* **BOCPD-t.** Bayesian online change-point detection (Adams and MacKay 2007) with a Student t observation model with ν = 3 fixed (the heaviest Student law with a finite variance) and an unknown variance on a grid (65 values from 0.04 to 25 times the unit variance in the simulations, log step 0.1; 71 values from e^-6 to e^4.5 times a predictable centre on real data, log step 0.15). The prior is E13's inverse gamma prior (α0 = 2, mean equal to the calibration mean square, or on real data the robust variance of the previous day) discretised on the grid; hazard 1 / A; runs of at least w steps are lumped into one class, which is exact for a constant hazard; alarm on the posterior log-odds of a run shorter than w (w = 250 as in E13); direction from the posterior mean variance of the short runs against the lumped class. The filter is the exact run-length recursion for this discrete model (tests: it equals the conjugate Gaussian filter of E13 when the t is replaced by a Gaussian, and the lumped recursion equals the unlumped one). It is calibrated as E13 calibrates BOCPD: threshold matched to the e-detector's rate on E13's own 4000 Gaussian calibration paths (1.55%), and the rule "posterior probability of a short run at least 0.5".
* **HKZ-type monitoring with a training sample.** The calibration window is the training sample of size m. Detector Q(m, k) = sum of Z over the first k monitored steps minus k times the training mean of Z, with Z = r² (the setting of the published procedure) or Z = X (our robust variant), standardised by sqrt(m) times a Bartlett long-run standard deviation of the training Z (bandwidth floor(m^(1/3)) = 12). Alarm when |Q(m, k)| / (σ sqrt(m)) ≥ c (1 + k/m) (k / (m + k))^γ, with γ = 0.25 (primary), 0 and 0.45; the sign gives the direction. A second boundary, sqrt(x (x - 1) (c² + log(x / (x - 1)))) with x = (m + k) / m, is that of OLS-CUSUM monitoring in the R package strucchange ("CSW-type"). Critical values: asymptotic, for open-ended monitoring at 5% (simulated quantile of sup_{0<t<1} |W(t)| / t^γ, 20,000 paths; for the strucchange boundary c solving 2 (1 - Φ(c) + c φ(c)) = 0.05), and calibrated on Gaussian data like the rules above (one critical value for |D|).
* **Reference.** The robust two-sided e-detector with a plug-in baseline, each side alarming at 2A, A = 7800 (1440-minute days and A = 28,800 on real data), computed with per-path decrease bets exactly as `VolatilityMonitor` does (unit test). On E13's paths E13's own version (decrease bets common to a batch) is also run, and reproduces E13's numbers exactly (checked in the script).

The equations could not be read, so a β-divergence version could not be built and checked; BOCPD-t is the Student t version that the referee allowed instead, and it is *not* their method.
* Horváth, Kokoszka and Zhang (2006), "Monitoring constancy of variance in conditionally heteroskedastic time series", Econometric Theory 22(3), 373 to 402 (Cambridge Core and RePEc listings): sequential monitoring of the unconditional variance with a training sample, with theory through a strong invariance principle for the squared observations. Their detector and boundary could not be read; we therefore call our rule "HKZ-type". Their boundary could not be read in the paper; the "CSW-type" boundary is the one implemented in strucchange (version 1.6-0, `R/monitoring.R`, read from the CRAN mirror on GitHub), which cites later work, so it is a sensitivity check and not a reproduction.

**Reading.**
* **The e-detector's own statistic with a calibrated threshold behaves like the e-detector.** Matched to the e-detector's Gaussian rate, SR betting has 1.6% under Gaussian data and 1.4% to 2.5% under the other i.i.d.-type laws, against 1.2% to 2.1% for the e-detector itself. With the Gaussian threshold for 5% it has 4.9% under Gaussian data but 6.1% to 7.6% under Student t, price noise and ticks. A threshold calibrated by simulation does not carry over to other laws; Ville's threshold has a guarantee of 5% for all of them (it is not attained, E9 and E16).
* **The Gaussian calibration of a CUSUM fails under fat tails even on the truncated statistic.** CUSUM on X matched to 1.3% reaches 5.0% to 8.2% under Student t3 and t5, and with the 5% threshold 14% to 22%. Truncation removes the effect of single large returns (jumps: 1.9% to 2.2%), but under a Student law the whole distribution of X / m0 changes shape (more small values and more values at the cap), and the CUSUM's threshold depends on that shape.
* **The bootstrap of the calibration window removes most of the dependence on the law.** Bootstrap thresholds keep SR betting at 1.5% to 1.8% and CUSUM small at 1.4% to 2.2% (target 1.3%), and at 5.1% to 6.0% and 4.9% to 6.3% for the 5% target, under all six i.i.d.-type laws. They are slightly above target even for Gaussian data, as expected: each side's threshold is an empirical quantile of 1000 replicates, and if the bootstrap law were the true law, the value that `np.quantile` returns there would have an expected tail probability of 0.75% instead of 0.65% per side at the 1.3% target, and 2.6% instead of 2.5% at 5%. The largest excess is under price noise (6.0% and 6.3% at 5%), whose noise makes successive returns dependent, which an i.i.d. bootstrap ignores. Under slow SV and GARCH the bootstrap rules, like all others, alarm on 46% to 58% and 82% to 90% of paths in the first day.
* **The HKZ-type rule on raw r² fails under fat tails; on X it holds.** With Gaussian critical values the rule on r² has 10.0% (t3) and 11.8% (jumps) against 1.3%, and 12.5% and 14.8% against 5%. Its standardisation by the long-run variance of r² presumes a finite fourth moment of the returns, which Student t3 lacks, and with jumps the squared returns are so skewed that early in monitoring, where the boundary is small, a few large values cross it. On X = min(r², τ²) the same rule stays at 1.1% to 1.6% and 4.7% to 5.1% under all six laws: X is bounded, and its Bartlett standardisation adapts to its law.
* **Asymptotic critical values say little about the first day.** For γ = 0 and 0.25 the open-ended critical value spends almost none of its 5% in the first 390 steps (0.0% to 0.2% under Gaussian data, price noise and ticks, and on X under all six laws), because 390 steps are only k / m = 0.2 of the training sample; for γ = 0.45 and the strucchange boundary the first day takes 2.8% to 5.1% (same laws). The open-ended check (`results/e21_hkz_open_end_check.csv`): over 20,000 steps (10 m) the γ = 0.25 rule on Gaussian r² has 4.6% against 4.3% for the limit over the same range.
* **GARCH: the HKZ-type rules alarm least** (21% to 52% in the first day against 82% to 92% for the betting and CUSUM rules). They compare the monitored mean with the training mean, standardised by a long-run variance that includes the clustering of the training sample, whereas the betting and CUSUM rules scale by m0 alone. Q21b shows what this costs in delay.

**Design.** E13's real-data design (Q13d) on the five Binance coins (BTC, ETH, XRP, SOL, DOGE): one-minute returns divided by the predictable intraday profile of E6b, 1 July to 31 December 2025 (184 days) and 1 January to 30 June 2025 (181 days, E13's held-out half-year). **Reading.**
* **Matched to the e-detector's alarm counts, the classical rules on its own statistic and design are as precise as the e-detector.** Over the eight comparisons per rule (two half-years, two bands, two directions; next-day outcome), SR betting with a calibrated threshold, CUSUM small and CUSUM medium are never significantly below the e-detector (week-clustered p ≥ 0.05 in all 24; differences from -5.0 to +3.0 points, mean -0.8 to -1.6), and SR betting is once significantly above it (Jan to Jun 2025, band 0, down alarms: 69.2% against 66.3%, week-clustered p = 0.019, z-test p = 0.35). This suggests that the e-detector's advantage over BOCPD and the VR scan in E13 comes from what these rules share (truncated squared returns measured against a baseline calibrated on a full day, and recalibration after each alarm), not from the betting form or from Ville's threshold. On the four-hour outcome the three rules are within -9.9 and +5.9 points of the e-detector, with significant differences in both directions (3 below, 2 above, of 24).
* **The HKZ-type rules are less precise**: below the e-detector in 13 of 16 comparisons (on r²: 8 of 8, 3 significant; on X: 5 of 8, 3 significant), by 2.8 (X) and 5.2 (r²) points on average.
* **BOCPD-t improves on E13's Gaussian BOCPD but stays below the e-detector.** At w = 250 (Jul to Dec 2025, band 0.25) it scores 69.0% and 70.7% against E13's 67.8% and 61.7%; with longer windows it comes closer (w = 1440: 69.7% and 77.0% against 78.5% and 80.5%). Over its 24 comparisons it is below the e-detector in 20, significantly in 14 (w = 250: 7 of 8; w = 720: 4; w = 1440: 3). On the four-hour outcome BOCPD-t with w = 250 is at or above the e-detector in all 8 comparisons, and significantly above it for down alarms (70.1% to 78.1% against 60.3% to 67.4%), as E13 found for short windows: such alarms mark changes over hours that need not last a day.
* **Gaussian-matched thresholds.** SR betting raises almost the same numbers of alarms as the e-detector (763 and 757 against 764 and 757), with almost the same precision. The CUSUMs raise 4% to 6% more alarms, with lower precision for down alarms in Jul to Dec 2025 (62.2% and 63.5% against 68.3%, week-clustered p < 0.001 and 0.006) and none in Jan to Jun. The HKZ-type rules raise far fewer alarms at their Gaussian critical values (on r²: 133 and 292; on X: 501 and 522), and with fewer alarms their up alarms are more often followed by a day above the forecast in Jan to Jun 2025 (86.7% and 81.6% against 74.8%, week-clustered p = 0.004 and 0.033; not in Jul to Dec). BOCPD-t at its Gaussian threshold raises 821 and 797 alarms, with lower precision (60.9% and 56.7% in Jul to Dec 2025, p < 0.001).
* **Matching precision.** Per series and band, |up count - target| + |down count - target| is 0 to 4 alarms for the restart rules (mean 0.75) and 0 to 9 for BOCPD-t (mean 1.2; E13's bisection, whose counts are also not monotone because of the pause after each alarm), out of 119 to 154 target alarms per series (`results/e21_real_h2_matched_thresholds.csv`, `results/e21_real_h1_matched_thresholds.csv`). Pooled over the coins, the numbers of scored alarms differ from the e-detector's by at most 8.
* **Multiplicity.** The p-values are not adjusted: this section makes 64 next-day comparisons at matched rates (8 rules, 8 each), so about 3 would fall below 0.05 by chance if no rule differed from the e-detector; 21 do, all but one of them for BOCPD-t and the HKZ-type rules.

### What was not done, and why

* **No β-divergence BOCPD.** Knoblauch, Jewson and Damoulas (2018) could not be read from this machine, and a version "of the type" whose construction we could not check against the paper would not answer the referee. BOCPD-t is a different, simpler robust BOCPD, named as such.
* **No exact HKZ or CSW procedure.** Neither paper could be read. The HKZ-type rule uses the verified boundary of monitoring in linear models applied to squared returns (the setting of HKZ), with critical values from simulation, so it is a procedure of their type and not their procedure; the CSW-type boundary is strucchange's.
* **No bootstrap on real data**, because rate matching sets the thresholds there (Q21d).
* **No block bootstrap.** The i.i.d. bootstrap was the pre-registered calibration; a block bootstrap, which would respect the dependence that price noise creates, was not tried.

### Post hoc decisions

* The count-matching search for the restart rules (iteration 2) was added after a timing run on BTC showed that bisection alone missed the targets by up to 25 alarms; it uses the alarm counts only, not the outcome, and was fixed before any real-data outcome of E21 was computed except in a smoke test of the pipeline on DOGE for two rules (iteration 4 of the notes), after which nothing was changed.
* The real-data Gaussian-matched threshold of BOCPD-t is computed with the real-data grid (decided before any real-data run).
* The rule for bold entries in the tables (95% interval above the target) and the choice of which rows to show were made when writing this section, after the results.
* Everything else (rules, reference values, γ, bandwidth, ν, grids, w, targets, seeds, numbers of paths, laws) was fixed in iteration 1.
