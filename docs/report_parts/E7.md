## E7. An Indian market application: Nifty 50 one-minute bars

**Data [REAL].** Nifty 50 index, one-minute bars in Indian Standard Time (IST), April 2017 to October 2026, loaded with `avmonitor.data.load_nifty` (source in `data/MANIFEST.csv`). A regular session runs 09:15 to 15:29, which is 375 bars and 374 intraday returns. Overnight returns are excluded, so the gap between one day's close and the next day's open never enters any statistic. An index has no bid-ask bounce (the jitter between buy and sell prices that affects a single traded price), but it is computed from the last traded prices of 50 stocks, so it can lag ("stale prices"); the lag-1 autocorrelation of minute returns is small but positive (0.027). Scripts: `experiments/e07_nifty.py` (Q7a, Q7b), `experiments/e07c_india_vix.py` (Q7c), `experiments/e07d_events.py` (Q7d). Notes on each iteration, including the failed ones: `docs/iterations/E7.md`.

**Cleaning.** A 50-stock index whose value does not change for 5 or more minutes in a row is not trading normally. That happened 76 times: two exchange outages (10 July 2017 and 24 February 2021), the two market-wide trading halts of 13 and 23 March 2020 (when the index fell 10% and trading stopped for 45 minutes), a stretch on 7 March 2022, and from 3 August 2026 the last 12 to 14 minutes of every session, which a web search ties to the start of NSE's Closing Auction Session that day (source in `data/events/nifty_events.csv`). These frozen minutes would look like "zero volatility", so they are set to missing, together with the first return after each frozen stretch (it spans the whole halt). The two outage sessions, where more than a quarter of the returns were frozen, are dropped, as are 6 short special sessions. Result: 2,361 sessions and 882,001 returns (`results/e07a_frozen_runs.csv`). As an outside check, NSE's own published daily high and low of the Nifty 50 match the minute data within 0.01% on about 94% of days (`results/e07c_nse_crosscheck.csv`).

### Q7a. What do Nifty minute returns look like, and how large is the truncation bias?

**Method.** The summary of Table 5 in Bapat (2026), plus a few extra columns, for Nifty and for the Bitstamp BTC/USD data of E5 (July to December 2025). Then integrated quarticity (IQ, the sum of the fourth power of volatility over a day, here with T = 1 session) per session with every estimator in `avmonitor.robust`, on raw and on deseasonalised returns (each return divided by the typical volatility of its minute of the day, see Q7b). The jump-robust MedRQ is the reference: the bias of a MAD-truncated estimator is its ratio to MedRQ. Truncation means clipping each return at c = 3 MADs (MAD = median absolute deviation, a robust measure of spread, scaled here to estimate the standard deviation).

| Series [REAL] | n | Min | Q1 | Mean | Q3 | Max | Zero returns | Robust sd | Excess kurtosis | Lag-1 autocorr. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nifty 50, Apr 2017 to Oct 2026 | 882,001 | -0.0155 | -1.60 × 10⁻⁴ | -6.3 × 10⁻⁷ | 1.62 × 10⁻⁴ | 0.0150 | 0.87% | 2.39 × 10⁻⁴ | 43 | 0.027 |
| Nifty 50, 2017 to 2021 | 437,474 | -0.0155 | -1.72 × 10⁻⁴ | -9.1 × 10⁻⁷ | 1.74 × 10⁻⁴ | 0.0150 | 1.30% | 2.56 × 10⁻⁴ | 47 | 0.026 |
| Nifty 50, 2022 to Oct 2026 | 444,527 | -0.0077 | -1.50 × 10⁻⁴ | -3.4 × 10⁻⁷ | 1.51 × 10⁻⁴ | 0.0087 | 0.45% | 2.23 × 10⁻⁴ | 18 | 0.029 |
| BTC/USD Bitstamp, Jul to Dec 2025 | 264,959 | -0.0171 | -2.11 × 10⁻⁴ | -7.7 × 10⁻⁷ | 2.13 × 10⁻⁴ | 0.0117 | 5.73% | 3.14 × 10⁻⁴ | 24 | 0.006 |
| BTC row of Bapat (2026) Table 5 [PUBLISHED] | | -0.0298 | -2.21 × 10⁻⁴ | -7.73 × 10⁻⁷ | 2.22 × 10⁻⁴ | 0.0314 | | | | |

The median is 0 in every row. All Nifty extremes come from 13 March 2020, around the first lower-circuit halt (minimum -1.55% at 10:35 and maximum +1.50% at 10:30 IST, just after trading resumed). The share of zero returns fell from 1.6% in 2017 to 0.3 to 0.6% from 2022 (`results/e07a_by_year.csv`).

Median ratio of daily IQ to MedRQ (`results/e07a_iq_summary.csv`):

| Estimator | Nifty raw | Nifty deseasonalised | BTC raw | BTC deseasonalised |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RQ (no protection against jumps) | 1.23 | 1.25 | 1.27 | 1.37 |
| TPQ / QPQ / MinRQ (jump-robust) | 1.04 / 0.98 / 1.04 | 1.02 / 0.97 / 1.03 | 0.96 / 0.88 / 1.02 | 0.96 / 0.87 / 1.01 |
| TRQ (drop returns above a shrinking threshold) | 0.91 | 0.99 | 0.64 | 0.69 |
| Plain kernel, h = 30 | **1.03** | 1.25 | 1.28 | 1.39 |
| Truncated RQ, scaled MAD, corrected for Gaussian clipping | **0.71** | **0.86** | **0.33** | **0.40** |
| Robust kernel, scaled MAD, corrected | 0.63 | 0.86 | 0.33 | 0.39 |
| Robust kernel, raw MAD (as in the paper) | 0.33 | 0.46 | 0.14 | 0.18 |
| Median share of returns clipped (Gaussian: 0.27%) | 2.4% | 1.6% | 4.5% | 3.8% |
| Median within-day excess kurtosis (Gaussian: 0) | 2.0 | 1.4 | 5.2 | 5.0 |

**Reading.**
* The MAD truncation bias is **much smaller on Nifty than on crypto**: corrected truncated RQ keeps 71% of the MedRQ level on Nifty against 33% on BTC (86% against 40% after deseasonalising).
* **Intraday seasonality** explains a good part of the Nifty bias. One MAD for the whole day is set by the calm middle of the session, so the volatile first minutes are clipped as if they were jumps. Removing the U-shape first raises the ratio from 0.71 to 0.86. On 24-hour crypto, deseasonalising helps less (0.33 to 0.40).
* **Fat tails** explain the rest, and they are the main reason crypto is worse: within a day BTC minute returns have excess kurtosis 5.0 to 5.2 against 1.4 to 2.0 for Nifty, and 3.8 to 4.5% of BTC returns are clipped against 1.6 to 2.4% for Nifty. The correction factor assumes Gaussian returns, so it cannot undo clipping of a fat-tailed distribution.
* **Exact zeros** matter little: with 5.7% zeros (BTC), the MAD falls so that the cut-off sits at 2.79 instead of 3 standard deviations, and the surviving fourth moment drops from 0.964 to 0.938 for Gaussian returns; for Nifty (0.87% zeros) the change is negligible (`results/e07a_zero_share_effect.csv`).
* **A side finding about the kernel.** On raw Nifty returns the plain kernel IQ (1.03) is far below RQ (1.23), while on deseasonalised returns the two agree (1.25). The averaged kernel estimator starts at minute h = 30, so the first half hour, the most volatile part of the session, gets only partial weight. On a session market this edge effect is a systematic downward bias, not a detail.

**Caveats.** MedRQ is a reference, not the truth. One crypto venue and six months against nine and a half years of one index.

### Q7b. The streaming monitor on Nifty

**Deseasonalisation check.** The package function `intraday_scale` was written for 24-hour crypto and smooths the profile around the clock, so on a 375-bar session it mixes the last minutes of the day (15:23 to 15:29) into the first ones. A session-aware profile (`session_profile` in `e07_nifty.py`: median of |r| per minute over the previous 20 sessions, 5-minute moving average cut at the open and the close) fixes this. Median |u| per minute relative to the whole session, 2017 to 2021 (`results/e07b_deseason_check.csv`; 1.00 is perfect):

| Profile | 09:16 | 09:16 to 09:20 | 15:25 to 15:29 |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | 2.84 | 2.45 | 0.84 |
| package `intraday_scale` (wraps around) | 1.84 | 1.44 | 0.61 |
| session-aware, used here | 1.15 | 1.04 | 1.03 |

The held-out years look the same (1.24, 1.04, 1.02). The function in `src` should get a `circular=False` option for session markets (see the hand-back notes).

**Settings, fixed before any monitor run.** One session is 375 steps. A = 375 / 0.05 = 7,500 per alarm level, so under the null (volatility equal to the baseline) the chance of any false alarm within a session is at most 5%. Truncation at c = 3 scaled MADs of the calibration window. Calibration 375 or 750 steps, null band b = 0, 0.25 or 0.5 (the band makes the monitor ignore changes in truncated variance smaller than a factor 1 ± b). Pre-registered primary configuration: calibration 750 (E4d found one session of calibration slightly above the bound) and band 0.25 (E4c). Development period 2017 to 2021 (1,170 sessions), held-out period 2022 to October 2026 (1,191 sessions). The monitor was run on the development period alone first (`results/*_devonly.csv`), then on everything. **Nothing was changed after looking at the held-out period.** Missing minutes are fed as missing (skipped), not as zeros.

**Validation.** For each alarm, compare the robust volatility of the next session (scaled median |u|) with the baseline the alarm was measured against. Control: 5,000 random minutes from the same run while it is monitoring, compared with the baseline in force at that minute. Intervals are Wilson 95% intervals. Because each alarm restarts calibration, no session has more than one alarm, so "alarms per session" is also the share of sessions with an alarm.

The random-time intervals are about ±1.5 percentage points. A second, simpler check compares the next session with the previous one: primary up alarms 73% (dev) and 78% (held-out) against 48% and 50% for all sessions; primary down alarms 67% and 65% against 52% and 50% (`results/e07b_monitor_summary.csv`, `results/e07b_control_sessions.csv`).

**Reading.**
* **Out of sample the monitor holds up.** With the pre-registered settings it raises about one alarm every four sessions in both periods (0.26 per session), and on held-out data 78% of up alarms are followed by a more volatile session than the baseline (47% at random times) and 76% of down alarms by a calmer one (53%). Every robust configuration beats its random-time control by 19 to 43 percentage points, in both periods.
* **The robust monitor's up alarms are clearly better than the Gaussian benchmark's** (78% against 61% held-out, calibration 750). The Gaussian detector raises about twice as many up alarms as down alarms and fires faster (median delay about 100 steps against about 400), but many of its up alarms are not followed by higher volatility, which fits alarms triggered by single large returns. For down alarms the two are similar on held-out data (76% each).
* **The band trades sensitivity for reliability**, as on crypto: band 0.5 halves the alarm rate and raises the hit rate of down alarms to 92 to 95% in three of four cells, but takes about 950 to 1,300 steps (2.5 to 3.5 sessions) to fire.
* **Alarms per session are far above the 5% false alarm bound** (0.14 to 0.57 for the robust monitor). This is not a broken guarantee. The guarantee is about periods where volatility really stays at the baseline, and Nifty volatility moves from day to day. The share of alarms in the first 30 minutes (3 to 7%) is below the 8% share of time, so the deseasonalised opening does not create spurious alarms.

**Caveats.** "Followed by higher volatility" is a sanity check, not ground truth: volatility clusters, so a detector reacting to a burst will look predictive. The random-time control removes part of that, not all. Validation alarms are not independent (neighbouring alarms share sessions). After each alarm the monitor recalibrates for 750 steps (2 sessions) and is blind meanwhile (see Q7d).

### Q7d. Event study with verified dates

**Event dates.** `data/events/nifty_events.csv` lists every date with a source URL. Official sites (RBI, Wikipedia) cannot be fetched from this environment, so every date was checked with a web search on 2 November 2026 and the cited URL is a search result that states the date. Included: 11 Union Budget days (2018 to 2026, including the interim budgets of 2019 and 2024 and the weekend special sessions of 1 February 2020, 2025 and 2026), 41 RBI monetary policy announcement days, the general election result days 23 May 2019 and 4 June 2024, and the two lower-circuit days of March 2020. 18 further RBI meetings that we looked for could not be tied to a dated source and were **left out** (by year: one in 2017, one in 2018, three in 2020, one in 2021, two in 2022, three in 2023, four in 2024, one in 2025, two in 2026). "COVID window" means every session from 2 to 31 March 2020 (our own calendar definition, 21 sessions). Monthly index derivative expiry days follow the rule in `data/events/nifty_expiry_rule.csv` (last Thursday of the month until August 2025, last Tuesday from September 2025, both verified by web search); in 5 months that weekday was a holiday, and those months were left out rather than guessing the replacement day. Ordinary sessions belong to none of these groups.

**Method.** Primary configuration (calibration 750, band 0.25). Share of sessions with at least one alarm, event type against ordinary sessions, Fisher exact test (two-sided). As a check, the same restricted to sessions where the monitor was already monitoring at 09:15 (otherwise it is still recalibrating after an earlier alarm and partly blind). An earlier version of this check used the share of the session spent monitoring and was wrong, because an alarm inside a session itself starts a recalibration (see the iteration notes).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Union Budget | 11 | **82%** (52 to 95) | 25% | 0.0001 | 73% | 13% | < 0.001 | 1.94 | 7 of 7 vs 41% (0.002) |
| RBI policy | 41 | 32% (20 to 47) | 25% | 0.36 | 22% | 13% | 0.10 | 1.06 | 46% vs 41% (0.68) |
| Election result | 2 | 2 of 2 | 25% | 0.06 | 2 of 2 | 13% | 0.02 | 4.46 | 2 of 2 vs 41% (0.17) |
| COVID window (March 2020) | 21 | 29% (14 to 50) | 25% | 0.62 | 19% | 13% | 0.34 | 2.67 | 44% vs 41% (1.0) |
| Monthly expiry | 110 | **38%** (30 to 48) | 25% | 0.002 | 23% | 13% | 0.006 | 1.05 | 63% vs 41% (0.002) |

Time of day of the alarms (`results/e07d_time_of_day.csv`): budget alarms fell between 10:18 and 12:58, 8 of 9 between 11:00 and 13:00 (the Lok Sabha record in the events file gives 11:00 as the start of the 2024 interim budget presentation). Both election day alarms came at the open (09:26 on 4 June 2024, 09:51 on 23 May 2019). RBI and ordinary alarms are spread across the day (median 11:24 and 12:19).

**Reading.**
* **Budget days are the clearest signal.** 9 of 11 budget sessions had an alarm, 8 of them up alarms, and every budget session where the monitor was watching from the open raised one. The alarms come in the hour or two after the speech starts. The two misses were sessions where the monitor was recalibrating at the open after an earlier alarm.
* **Election results**: both days were flagged at the open; on 4 June 2024 the robust volatility was 6.3 times the median of the previous 20 sessions.
* **RBI policy days are not special** for this monitor: 32% against 25%, not significant. Their volatility is close to the recent norm (median 1.06 times the previous 20 sessions, against 0.97 on ordinary days), so there is little to detect.
* **Monthly expiry days have more alarms** (38% against 25%, p = 0.002), mostly up alarms. A plausible reason is that the intraday profile averages all weekdays, so the expiry-day pattern looks like a level change; the profile could be conditioned on expiry days.
* **The March 2020 crash shows a limit of the design.** Volatility in that window was 2.7 times the previous 20 sessions, yet only 6 of 21 sessions had an alarm. After each alarm the monitor recalibrates on the new, already high level, and then needs a further change to alarm again; 12 of the 21 sessions started in recalibration. The monitor answers "has volatility changed from the recent baseline?", not "is volatility high?". Figure `results/figures/e07_nifty_alarms_crises.png` shows the alarms over February to April 2020 and around the June 2024 election (up alarm at 09:26 on 4 June, then two blind sessions during the fall and rebound).

**Caveats.** Small samples (11 budget days, 2 election days). Event sessions are partly predictable in time, so a practical system would simply watch them; the test shows the monitor reacts to real information, not that it forecasts it. Figure: `results/figures/e07_nifty_event_alarm_rates.png`.
