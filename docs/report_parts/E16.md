## E16. Adversarial null search: how close to the bound can a legitimate adversary get, and where does the guarantee break?

Script `experiments/e16_adversarial_null.py`; outputs `results/e16*.csv` and `results/figures/e16_*.png`; tests `tests/test_e16_adversarial.py`; iteration notes `docs/iterations/E16.md`. All results **[SYNTHETIC]**. Seeds are fixed in the script, and every reported rate comes from seeds that the search never saw.

**Question.** Theorem B says that the default two-sided detector raises a false alarm within m steps with probability at most m / A, whenever E[X_t | past] = m0 with the true m0. E9 found it about 4 times conservative for four natural laws (1.1% to 1.4% per day against 5%). How close to m / A can a legitimate adversary, one that satisfies every condition of the theorem, push the full default detector, including laws that depend on the past and on the detector's own state? And outside the theorem's conditions, where exactly does the bound fail, by how much, and does each failure match the condition that was broken?

**Set-up.** The full default detector: X_t = min(r_t², τ²), τ = 3 times the scaled MAD, six increase bets 0.02 to 0.5, five decrease bets 0.1 to 0.95 of 1 / (B - 1) with B = τ² / m0, Shiryaev-Roberts statistics, each side alarming at 2A, A = 7800. The bound is 5% for one 390-step day and 25% for five days (1950 steps). Inside the class only the law of Y_t = X_t / m0 matters: Y_t lies in [0, B] and must have conditional mean exactly 1. At each step both statistics are affine in Y_t, so a two-point law with mean 1 is as good as any law for the adversary (the concave envelope argument of Lemma 2a in `docs/theory_delay.md`), and the adversary's whole choice is a pair of points at each step, possibly depending on everything seen so far. With τ = 3 times the scaled MAD of the law itself and a positive median absolute return, B can only lie between 1.90 and 39.6 (half the returns are at least the median in size, so m0 is at least half the squared median; the other half are at most the median, so m0 is at most the average of the squared median and τ²; and τ² / median² = (3 × 1.4826)² = 19.78); the Gaussian value is 9.045. When more than half of the returns are zero, the monitor's fallback (the median of the nonzero absolute returns) allows larger B, up to 149 for the price ticks of Q16f; bold play at B = 100 to 1,000 stays below its rates at B = 2 (Q16b). Under the theorem τ may be any level fixed in advance, so any B above 1 is legitimate.

**Search and reporting.** Grid search, then Nelder-Mead, on common random numbers (the same uniforms for every candidate, so the objective is a fixed function of the parameters); 20,000 paths at one day and 6,000 to 8,000 at five days. The search maximum is biased upwards (iteration 2 of the notes; the in-sample values are kept in the CSV files as `train_rate`), so the best law of each family is re-run on fresh seeds: 200,000 paths at one day and 60,000 at five days (102,560 and 41,020 for shape switching), with 95% Wilson intervals. "Above the bound" means that the lower end of the interval is above it. The tests check that every searched law keeps the conditional mean at 1, that bold play lands on the threshold, that each return law uses its own true τ and m0, that the price-tick control keeps the values and removes the dependence, that the floor constant of Q16c is the smallest value of the statistic, and that the vectorised detector matches `VolatilityMonitor` alarm for alarm.

**Summary.**
* **Inside the class the bound always holds, with room to spare.** No law found, i.i.d., dependent or adaptive, uses much more than half of the bound at one day: the worst is bold play, an adversary that watches the detector and uses the widest legal spread, at 2.62% per day (95% interval 2.50% to 2.75%, B = 2) against 5%; at the Gaussian B it gives 2.40% (2.34% to 2.47%). Over five days the worst is 18.1% (17.8% to 18.5%) against 25%. Not one interval reaches the bound.
* **The adversary removes about half of E9's slack; under every law tried, the rest is wealth left on paths that never alarm.** The Gaussian null uses 0.19 of the bound in one day (0.97%); the worst legitimate law uses 0.52, so E9's factor of about 4 to 5 shrinks to about 2 (at five days from 2.0 to 1.4). Under bold play stopping and overshoot cost nothing; what is left is expected wealth held on paths that never alarm (about half of it), 60% of it by the decrease bets and 36% by the three smallest increase bets, which did not reach 2A within a day under any law tried while the same draws also serve the other bets; whether some null law can move this wealth onto alarmed paths is open. What is proved is much weaker: no null law can exceed 0.98 of the bound at one day (Q16c).
* **Outside the class the bound fails exactly where a stated condition fails.** GARCH clustering: above the bound in 43 of 64 cells at one day, ordered by the drift of the conditional mean (Spearman 0.99), from a mean drift of 3.9% at one day (1.6% over five days); betting against the true conditional mean on the same paths stays at or below 0.24 of the bound (0.56 at five days). Plug-in baseline: 41% to 42% over five days with i.i.d. returns after a 390-step calibration, up to 32.5% in one day under a shape change that keeps the conditional mean, while the same τ-hat with the true mean never exceeds 0.59 of the bound on fresh paths (0.63 in the search) and does not alarm at all under that shape change. Price-level ticks: above the bound only from a tick of 4 sd over five days (6 sd for one day), where the conditional mean after an unchanged price drops to 0.88 of m0; the same returns drawn independently, with the same τ and m0, stay at or below 0.32 of the bound at one day and 0.57 at five days (7.4% over five days at 4 sd, against 28.8%). None of these is a failure of Theorem B.

**Reading.** No law or policy comes near the bound: the largest ratio in the table is 0.48 at one day and 0.71 at five days, and the Monte Carlo intervals are narrow (about plus or minus 0.07 points at one day). Among i.i.d. laws the worst are two-point laws at the edges, {0, B} with a small B (2.14% at B = 2.05); the worst return law is a scale mixture with almost half the returns at 13 times the scale (2.07%), and heavy tails as such matter less (Student t with ν = 2.1: 1.66%; ticks: 1.49%). Holding Y = 1 first and then playing bold changes little (2.39% after a hold of 50 steps; 17.8% after a hold of 1000 steps at five days). Switching between shapes at random times, with the conditional mean held at 1, is no worse for the detector than the i.i.d. law {0, B} (1.89% and 15.0%).

### Q16c. Where the remaining slack sits

**Method.** The exact optional-stopping identity of `docs/theory_delay.md` (8.1) for the two-sided statistic N = (M↑ + M↓) / 2: P(alarm within m) = (m / A) × F1 × F2 × F3, with F1 the stopping factor, F2 the share of expected wealth that sits on paths that alarmed (one minus the "stranded" wealth on paths that did not), and F3 = A / E[N at the alarm], the overshoot. 100,000 fresh paths of one day for each law (`e16a_slack.csv`). Then bold play on single bets at level A and on each side of the default grid alone at 2A (bound m / (2A)), against E11's dynamic programme (DP), whose values are lower bounds on the worst case over the whole null class (`e16a_single_bet_check.csv`, 41,020 fresh paths each). Entries are rate divided by the bound. Finally, what can be proved, and the same programme on finer wealth grids (`e16a_floor_and_dp.csv`, part a6, no random draws).

| adaptive: bold play | 2.39% (2.29 to 2.48) | 0.48 | 0.99 | 0.49 | 1.00 | 0.49 | 60% | 36% |

**Reading.** Bold play removes the overshoot completely (F3 rises from 0.79 to 1.00: at an alarm the two sides together hold 2A instead of about 1.27 times 2A) and doubles F2 relative to Gaussian data (0.25 to 0.49), which the i.i.d. law {0, B} already does. What is left is F2: half of the expected wealth at the end of the day sits on paths that did not alarm, 60% of it in the five decrease bets and 36% in the increase bets 0.02, 0.05 and 0.1. Every Y_t has to serve all eleven bets at once: a draw at B multiplies the increase wealth but cuts the decrease wealth, a draw at 0 does the opposite, and, as E11 found for Gaussian data, the small increase bets are held back by their size and the decrease bets by the cap 1 / (B - 1), so under the laws tried they keep their expected wealth without reaching 2A within a day. For a single bet the same policy is about as strong as the best adversary known: it matches E11's dynamic programme within 0.03 of the bound for the bets 0.5 and 0.2 and the decrease bet at one to ten days (at a quarter of a day it is 0.08 below it for the decrease bet), and for the bet 0.05 it does better than E11's values (0.26 against 0.18 at one day, 0.77 against 0.69 at five days). Those values are lower bounds computed on a 700-point log grid of wealth states, which rounds the step M → M + 1 (a draw at Y = 1, the way a small bet builds its wealth) back down to M once M exceeds about 77. On finer grids the programme's value for the bet 0.05 rises to 0.205, 0.220 and 0.232 of the bound at one day (1,400, 2,800 and 5,600 states), still below bold play's 0.26, and to 0.77 at five days and 0.81 at ten days (2,800 states), level with bold play at five days (0.77, interval 0.75 to 0.78) and above it at ten (0.75). So the bound is nearly sharp for the single bets 0.5 and 0.2 and the decrease bet at one day or more, while no law tried takes the mixture of eleven bets on two sides above about half of it within a day; a provably sharper threshold for the default grid would need a proof that F2 stays well below 1 for every null law, which we do not have (the smallest value the two-sided statistic can take on a day without an alarm, c = 8.3, proves only P(false alarm within m) ≤ (m - c) / (A - c), 4.90% at one day, 0.98 of the bound: on such a day every increase bet is at least where draws of 0 at every step would leave it and every decrease bet at least where draws of B would, and optional stopping gives A P + c (1 - P) ≤ m).

### Q16d. Outside the class: GARCH clustering (the conditional mean drifts)

**Method.** GARCH(1,1) returns with Gaussian innovations and unit unconditional variance, on a grid of 8 persistences α + β (0.5 to 0.999, half-lives of the volatility shocks 1 to 693 steps) and 8 values of α (0.002 to 0.3): 64 cells, 6,000 paths of five days each. τ and m0 are the true *unconditional* values (from 400 stationary paths of 10,000 steps per cell), as in E9. Here the conditional mean μ_t = E[X_t | past] = σ_t² h(τ / σ_t), with h(k) = E[min(Z², k²)] for a standard normal Z, is known exactly, which gives two things on the same paths: the size of the broken assumption, written as the mean absolute drift E|μ_t / m0 - 1|, and the false alarm rate of the same detector betting against the true μ_t (its decrease bets rescaled each step), which is back inside the class (`e16b_garch.csv`). Bold cells: the lower end of the 95% interval is above the bound.

**Reading.** The failure follows the drift of the conditional mean. At one day the bound is exceeded in every cell whose mean absolute drift is above 5.6% and in none whose drift is below 3.9%; at five days the cut lies between 1.6% and 2.5%. Persistence matters at a given drift: a drift of 5.6% that dies out within a step (α + β = 0.5, α = 0.05) gives 0.60 of the bound in one day, a drift of 3.9% with a half-life of 69 steps (α + β = 0.99, α = 0.005) gives 1.11, because the bets compound over a run of steps on the same side of m0. Across the 64 cells the drift alone orders the one-day ratio almost perfectly (Spearman correlation 0.99). Both sides raise alarms (the increase side gives 33% to 86% of the alarms in the failing cells at one day), because calm spells push μ_t below m0 just as bursts push it above. The control settles the attribution: betting against the true μ_t, the same detector on the same paths never exceeds 0.24 of the bound at one day (at most 1.2% of days) or 0.56 at five days (at most 14.1%), including in the cells where the unconditional detector alarms on every path. So each failure is the stated condition failing (E[X_t | past] is not m0), not the proof. A mean drift of 4% is small: it is what α = 0.005 with a half-life of 69 steps (about an hour of one-minute bars) produces, and it is enough. This sharpens E9's finding; E10's forecast-relative monitor is the remedy.

### Q16e. Outside the class: a plug-in baseline (the baseline is estimated)

**Method.** The monitor's own plug-in: τ-hat = 3 times the scaled MAD and m-hat = the mean of min(r², τ-hat²) over a calibration window of 390 or 1440 returns, then five days of monitoring; the returns are i.i.d. throughout, so the null holds exactly and only the baseline is estimated. Three detectors run on the same paths: the plug-in (τ-hat, m-hat); a "τ-hat oracle" that keeps τ-hat but bets against the true mean m(τ-hat) of the law at that level, which is inside the class; and the full oracle (true τ and m0). Search: 37 laws per calibration length (Gaussian; scale mixtures of two normals, 5 weights times 5 scale ratios; Student t, ν = 2.2 to 10; returns rounded to a tick of 0.1 to 0.9 sd) on 6,000 paths, then the worst law of each family re-run on 30,000 fresh paths (`e16b_plugin_search.csv`, `e16b_plugin.csv`). **Reading.** With i.i.d. returns no law found pushes the one-day rate significantly above 5% (the worst, a scale mixture with 10% of returns at 5 times the scale, gives 5.10%, interval 4.86% to 5.36%, with a 390-step calibration), but every law searched gives 40% to 44% within five days against 25% (the worst of each family 41% to 42% on fresh paths), as E14 found; a 1440-step calibration is not significantly above the bound for any law (fresh paths: at most 24.5%, interval 24.1% to 25.0%, at five days; search: at most 25.3% on 6,000 paths). The τ-hat oracle matches the full oracle within noise everywhere (never above 0.59 of the bound on the fresh paths; 0.63 at most in the 6,000-path search), so estimating the truncation level is harmless and the excess comes entirely from m-hat. The deciles of the baseline error m-hat / m(τ-hat) - 1 show where: with a 390-step calibration and q = 1, the one-day rate is 16.0% in the decile where m-hat is most too low (error -29% to -9%) and 7.5% in the decile where it is most too high, but 0.8% to 1.7% in the four middle deciles (4 to 7; 0.8% to 1.1% in deciles 5 to 7, close to the oracle's 0.9%). The shape adversary shows that the plug-in has no distribution-free rate at all: the less noise the monitored law has, the faster a fixed baseline error compounds, up to 32.5% of days within one day (85.8% within five days) at q = 0 with a 390-step calibration, and 16.1% (73.1%) with 1440 steps, while the τ-hat oracle on the same paths never alarms (with Y = 1 at every step its statistic grows by exactly one per step). This is Proposition A.6 (E14) in numbers: a bound of the form C m / A cannot hold for the plug-in, and the failure is the estimated baseline, the condition Theorem B states.

### Q16f. Outside the class: price-level ticks (returns become dependent)

**Method.** The efficient log price is a Gaussian random walk (one sd per step); the observed price is rounded to a tick h, so returns are differences of rounded prices and are dependent (a bid-ask-bounce-like pattern: a move up is often followed by a move back). τ (the protocol rule: 3 times the scaled MAD, with the median of the nonzero absolute returns when more than half are zero) and m0 are the true unconditional values from 1,000 paths of 10,000 steps per tick; 10,000 monitored paths per tick. Since τ and m0 are the true unconditional values, the only condition of Theorem B that can fail is the conditional mean. The control inside the class draws the returns i.i.d. from the marginal law of the price-rounded returns (pooled over the same 1,000 long paths, so the same values with the same frequencies), with the same τ and m0, on 10,000 paths of its own seeds: only the dependence is removed. The older twin rounds each return independently to the same tick; it is inside the class too (with its own exact τ and m0), but it is a different law, with far more zero returns and a much larger B (95% zeros and B = 435 at a tick of 4 sd), so it checks the tick grid, not the values. ### Reading

Inside the class, Theorem B held in every one of the laws and policies tried, and the strongest adversary found, one that sees the detector's state, uses about half of the bound at one day (0.52) and 0.73 of it at five days. That is 2.7 times what Gaussian data use at one day (0.19) and 1.5 times at five days (0.50), so the bound is not as loose against a worst case as E9's factor of about 4 suggested for natural laws: an adversary inside the class can take that factor down to about 2 at one day. Under every law we tried, the rest of the slack is wealth left in the decrease bets and the small increase bets, which did not use their share of the budget within a day while one draw has to serve all eleven bets on both sides at once; we have not shown that no null law can do better (the only proved limit is 0.98 of the bound at one day, Q16c). For the single bets 0.5 and 0.2 and the decrease bet the same adversary comes within a few hundredths of the bound fraction reached by E11's dynamic programme, so the bound is nearly sharp for these bets at one day or more; for the bet 0.05 the best adversary known reaches only 0.26 of the bound in a day.

Outside the class every failure lines up with the condition that was broken, and each has a control inside the class that stays below the bound (on the same paths for GARCH and the plug-in; for price ticks the same values drawn independently, with the same τ and m0): GARCH clustering breaks the conditional mean condition (a mean drift of about 4% suffices when it persists for an hour; betting against the true conditional mean restores the bound); a plug-in baseline breaks the "true baseline" condition (the excess is entirely the error of m-hat, not of τ-hat, and a law with less noise after calibration makes it as large as 32.5% in one day); price-level ticks break the conditional mean condition once the tick is several times the one-step volatility (the same values drawn independently stay at or below 0.57 of the bound). These are failures of the assumptions, not of the theorem. For practice: the monitor's guarantee is only as good as its baseline and the absence of persistent clustering relative to that baseline, which is why E8 (a confidence-sequence baseline), E10 (a forecast-relative null) and E14 (route B, no baseline) exist.

### Caveats

* A search finds lower bounds on the worst case, never the worst case itself: a law we did not try could do better. The only bound below m / A that is proved is the floor bound of Q16c (0.98 of the bound at one day). The policy families (bold play, an 8-parameter family around it, hold-then-bold) are broad but not exhaustive, and an exact computation over the 11-dimensional state of the default grid was not attempted.
* All rates are Monte Carlo estimates on fresh seeds; the in-sample search values (`train_rate`) are biased upwards, by up to 0.45 points at one day for the 8-parameter policy, and are not reported as results. A few fresh seeds are shared by different families, which makes those comparisons paired, not independent; part b3 (price ticks) reuses the seeds of the plug-in search with a 1440-step calibration, and since the two parts are unrelated this only makes them dependent.
* GARCH uses Gaussian innovations, so that the conditional mean is exact; the map is for A = 7800 and the default grid. The cut points in drift (3.9% to 5.6% at one day, 1.6% to 2.5% at five days) are read off a grid of 64 cells.
* The plug-in part searched four i.i.d. families and one shape adversary that uses only the calibration window; the vectorised plug-in does not extend a calibration window whose median absolute return is zero (the monitor does), so ticks above 0.9 sd were left out there.
* The price-tick model is a Gaussian random walk rounded to a fixed grid, with true unconditional τ and m0; real order books add other effects.
* Everything is synthetic.
