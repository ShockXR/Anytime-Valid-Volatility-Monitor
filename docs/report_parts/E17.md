## E17. One monitor for 1, 5 and 15 minutes: averaging e-detectors across sampling frequencies

Script `experiments/e17_multifrequency.py` (parts `baselines`, `levels`, `null`, `change`, `real`, `figures`, each runnable alone); outputs `results/e17_*.csv` and `results/figures/e17_delays.png`. Theory in `docs/theory_multifreq.md`. Code: `src/avmonitor/multifreq.py` (`MultiFrequencyMonitor`, `proved_bound`, `level_for_budget`, `handicap`); tests `tests/test_multifreq.py`. Seeds are fixed in the script. The real-data settings were written into the script's docstring and committed before the real part was run for the first time. Iteration notes, including a design change made after seeing results: `docs/iterations/E17.md`.

**Question.** The monitor bets on one-minute returns. Some volatility changes are easier to see in 5-minute or 15-minute returns: when one-minute prices carry noise (for example from the bid-ask bounce, ALT1), or when returns become autocorrelated, which moves the variance of a block but not the one-minute variance. Instead of choosing one sampling frequency, we can run an e-detector at each frequency and average them (an average of e-values is an e-value: Vovk and Wang 2021). Does the average keep a proved false alarm bound? Does it beat each single frequency? If not, is it at least never much worse than the best one, and does it help under price noise?

**Method.**

* **Theory** (`docs/theory_multifreq.md`). Frequency k sums the one-minute returns of non-overlapping k-minute blocks, clips the squared block return at `τ_k² = (3 × scaled MAD of k-minute returns)²` and runs its own pair of betting detectors (increase and decrease) over blocks, exactly like the one-minute monitor.
  * *Each frequency needs its own null.* The mean of the clipped squared block return, given everything seen up to the start of the block, must stay below the increase baseline (and above the decrease baseline). If one-minute returns are independent with one fixed distribution (this allows fat tails, jumps and price ticks), the one-minute null implies this for every k. In general it does not: section 2.1 of the note gives returns that satisfy the one-minute null but whose 5-minute blocks have mean square 17.7 instead of 5.
  * *The obvious guarantee is false.* A k-minute detector changes only at the end of each block. One minute before a block ends, most of its next bet is already decided by the returns seen so far, so on the one-minute clock it is not a fair bet. The usual proof of `P(false alarm within m minutes) ≤ Σ_k w_k (m / k) / A` for the plain weighted average, checked every minute, therefore fails. Proposition 2 gives a two-minute example in which the expected value of the stopped detector exceeds what the proof needs by 0.484 λ, where λ is the bet size. Combining e-processes that live on different information flows is a known difficulty (Choe and Ramdas, arXiv:2402.09698).
  * *The fix: a certified average.* Between its block ends, a coarse detector is counted only at the smallest value its wealth can take at the next block end. With `M` its value at the last block end and `ℓ` the smallest possible bet factor (`1 - λ` for an increase bet, `1 - φ` for a decrease bet), this credit is `min(M, ℓ (M + 1))`. At block ends nothing is lost. **Theorem 1:** with each side alarming at 2A, `P(false alarm within m minutes) ≤ Σ_k w_k ⌈m / k⌉ / A`, which is smaller than the one-minute monitor's `m / A`, and the mean time to the first false alarm is at least `(A - κ) / ρ` with `ρ = Σ_k w_k / k` and `κ = Σ_k w_k (1 - 1/k)`. Corollary 4 extends the bound to recalibration after every alarm. Theorem 3 proves weaker bounds without certification: `Σ_k w_k ⌊m / k⌋ / A` for the weighted maximum, and three times more than Theorem 1 for the plain average with balanced weights.
  * *The price of not knowing the frequency.* If only frequency j sees the change, the average must push that detector to `h_j` times the threshold it would need alone (the handicap). Proposition 3: for any weights the largest handicap is at least the number of frequencies (here 3), the same price as splitting the budget three ways (Bonferroni). Equality holds for "balanced" weights proportional to k.
* **Monitors** (fixed before any run). Every monitor's level A is set so that its proved bound is 5% per 1,440-minute day (Q17a). Each frequency alone; certified averages with **equal** weights (1/3 each), **balanced** weights (proportional to k) and **sqrt** weights (proportional to √k); the **plain average checked every minute** with balanced weights at the certified level, where Theorem 3 proves only 15% (to see whether certification matters in practice); the **weighted maximum** with balanced weights; and the balanced average of **5 and 15 minutes only**. Under price noise also **gap** versions, in which each coarse block leaves out its first one-minute return so that consecutive blocks share no noisy price; under independent noise, gap blocks are independent and Theorem 1 applies exactly. All with c = 3 and the default bet grids; band 0 in the synthetic parts.
* **[SYNTHETIC] laws.** One-minute returns with unit variance in the efficient price: Gaussian; Student t with 3 degrees of freedom (unit variance); jumps (probability 0.005 per minute, size N(0, 8²)); and price noise `r_t = e_t + u_t - u_{t-1}` with Gaussian `e_t` and noise `u_t ~ N(0, γ²)`, γ = 0.5, 1 and 2 (ALT1's model). Under price noise, consecutive one-minute returns are correlated (-γ² / (1 + 2γ²): -0.17, -0.33 and -0.44), so no monitor that uses one-minute returns or plain coarse blocks has a proof there. The true truncation levels and baselines of every law, frequency and block type come from 2 million simulated blocks each (`e17_true_baselines.csv`).
* **False alarms.** 10,000 monitored days of 1,440 minutes per cell, with the true baseline and with the plug-in baseline estimated on the 1,440 minutes before (all block phases of the calibration window are used for the coarse frequencies).
* **Changes** (true baseline, 2,000 paths per cell). The change comes at monitored minute 247, which is not a multiple of 5 or 15, so coarse blocks straddle it. Persistent changes of the standard deviation (×1.25, ×1.5, ×2, ×0.8, ×2/3, ×0.5), watched for 1,440 minutes; bursts (×2 or ×3 lasting 15 or 60 minutes), detected only if the alarm comes before the burst ends plus 15 minutes; and a **trend**: the lag-one autocorrelation of the efficient returns rises from 0 to 0.3 while the one-minute variance stays the same, so only block variances move (by factors 1.61 at 5 minutes and 1.78 at 15 minutes, from the closed form for this autocorrelation). A path counts as detected if its first alarm comes after the change and in the right direction; a false alarm before the change (at most 1.7% of paths in any cell) or an alarm in the wrong direction is a miss. The delay is counted in minutes from the change.
* **[REAL] five coins.** Binance spot BTC, ETH, XRP, SOL and DOGE one-minute bars, 1 July to 31 December 2025, deseasonalised exactly as in E6 (`e06b_five_coins_monitor.prepare`). Monitors: the three singles, the three certified averages and the balanced maximum, with plain blocks and E6's band 0.25, calibration 1,440 minutes and recalibration after every alarm. Validation exactly as E6 (Q6d, Q6e): the share of up alarms followed by higher robust volatility over the next 1,440 minutes than the one-minute baseline of the calibration window (down alarms: lower), with 95% Wilson intervals and the matched-delay random-time control; and the share of alarms beaten by (up) or beating (down) an EWMA volatility forecast, against 3,000 random times per coin. Descriptive only: how much of the combined statistic comes from each frequency at the alarm, and the share of alarms with a same-direction one-minute alarm within 60 minutes.

**Summary.**

* **The obvious guarantee is false, and a repaired one is proved.** The plain every-minute average has no proof from the standard argument (Proposition 2). The certified average has `P(false alarm within m) ≤ Σ_k w_k ⌈m / k⌉ / A` (Theorem 1). We spend that gain by lowering A until the bound is again 5% per day.
* **No combination beats the best single frequency in any i.i.d. design (at best it ties), but the good ones stay close to it.** For persistent changes the one-minute detector is the fastest. The equal-weight average is 0% to 5% slower, the sqrt average 4% to 10% and the balanced average 11% to 18%, in line with the handicaps of Proposition 3. On the trend, which the one-minute detector almost never sees (3.8% detected for Gaussian returns), the averages detect 95.5% to 97.3% at 1.17 to 1.32 times the 5-minute median delay. **No single frequency handles both short bursts and the trend; every average handles both reasonably well.**
* **Certification costs nothing visible.** The plain average checked every minute, which has only a 15% proved bound at this level, has the same false alarm rates (0.78% against 0.77% for Gaussian returns) and the same delays as the certified one. The factor 3 in Theorem 3 is a gap in the proof, not in behaviour, in these designs.
* **Price noise.** The one-minute detector is invalid under noise (6.88% and 12.06% per day at γ = 1 and 2, against a 5% target), and every average that contains it inherits part of the excess (balanced 3.09% and 6.50%, against 0.77% for Gaussian returns). The only combination with a proof under noise, the gap average of 5 and 15 minutes (0.54% and 0.52%), detects increases better than either valid single frequency at γ = 2 (×1.25: 28.1% against 21.4% and 25.3%; ×1.5: 93.3% against 85.5% and 82.8%) but decreases worse than the 5-minute gap detector alone (×0.5: 47.2% against 56.5%). It helps for increases, not for decreases.
* **Estimated baselines.** The plug-in baseline raises every false alarm rate (to 5.47% for the one-minute detector on Gaussian returns, significantly above 5%). The coarse detectors stay lower (5 minutes 2.45%, 15 minutes 1.66%), and the balanced average stays below 5% on all i.i.d. laws (3.66% to 4.54%).
* **[REAL] On five coins the averages behave like the one-minute monitor.** At alarms, the one-minute detector makes up 98.4% to 99.7% of the combined statistic on average. Alarm rates are 0.65 to 0.68 per day (one-minute monitor 0.68), and the validated shares are equal or 1 to 4 points lower (up 78% to 80% against 80%; down 84% to 88% against 88%). The 5-minute and 15-minute detectors alone alarm less often (0.43 and 0.26 per day) and their alarms are confirmed more often (up 90% and 95%, down 95% and 96%), but this is not a comparison at equal alarm rates.

**Reading.** Equal weights barely handicap the one-minute detector (1.27) but handicap the 15-minute one by 19. Balanced weights spread the price evenly (3 for each), which Proposition 3 shows is the smallest possible worst case. A handicap h adds about log h to the logarithm of the threshold, which is what drives the delay: log 3 = 1.1 against log(2 × 28,800) = 11.0 for the one-minute detector, so we expect the balanced average to be about 10% slower than the best frequency when that frequency carries all the evidence.

* **Moderate noise (γ = 1): the one-minute returns still carry most of the information, but no guarantee.** The one-minute detector is still the fastest single frequency for every change except the trend, but it alarms on 6.88% of null days. Among the proved monitors, the 5-minute gap detector is the best single frequency. The proved 5 and 15 average matches its detection on increases (78.7% against 79.2% at ×1.25; 100% at ×1.5 and ×2, 10 to 25 minutes later), loses on decreases (×0.8: 36.1% against 48.9%) and wins on the trend (83.5% against 76.3% and 71.2%).
* **Strong noise (γ = 2): coarse frequencies win, and the 5 and 15 average helps for increases.** The one-minute detector is no longer the best single frequency: the plain 5-minute detector detects more for ×1.25, ×1.5, ×2, ×2/3 and ×0.5 (for example ×0.5: 75.1% against 42.8%) and the plain 15-minute detector for the trend, while the one-minute detector alarms on 12.06% of null days. * **Gap blocks buy the proof at a cost in power.** Leaving out one return in five makes the 5-minute detector slower (γ = 2, ×1.25: 21.4% against 37.4% with plain blocks). The plain-block 5 and 15 average has no proof but stays at 1.56% and detects more (×1.25: 39.6%; trend 53.2%). Neighbouring plain blocks share one noisy price, but the conditional mean of a block's square moves with the previous block only by the factor `γ⁴ / (k + 2γ²)²` (section 2.1 of the note): 0.020 at 5 minutes and 0.0035 at 15 minutes when γ = 1, 0.09 and 0.03 when γ = 2. This may explain why its false alarm rate stays low; it is not a proof.

**Reading.**

* **On these coins the combination is the one-minute monitor with a small delay.** At alarms, the one-minute detector makes up 98.4% to 99.7% of the combined statistic on average, and 72% to 94% of the averages' alarms have a one-minute alarm in the same direction within 60 minutes before or after. The balanced average alarms a median of 6 to 10 minutes after the matching one-minute alarm (per coin), the equal average 1 to 2 minutes after. Its validation figures are the same as the one-minute monitor's or slightly lower (balanced: up 79% against 80%, down 84% against 88%, EWMA 75% and 77% against 78% and 80%).
* **This fits the synthetic results.** Real one-minute returns on these coins have little of the noise that would favour coarse frequencies: E13 measured lag-one autocorrelations of deseasonalised one-minute returns of -0.016 to +0.027 for BTC, ETH, XRP and SOL and +0.132 for DOGE (`results/e13d_real_crypto_random.csv`), far from the -0.33 of γ = 1. So the situation is like the i.i.d. rows of Q17c, where the average follows the one-minute detector.
* **The coarse detectors alone are more selective, not shown to be better.** The 5-minute and 15-minute alarms are confirmed more often (up 90% and 95%, down 95% and 96%) but come only 0.43 and 0.26 times per day, against 0.68. E6 found the same trade-off when the band is widened, so these shares cannot be compared with the one-minute monitor's at different alarm rates. The 15-minute down alarms beat the EWMA forecast less often (72%) than the one-minute ones (80%).

**Answers.** (1) *Does the combination beat each single frequency?* Not in any i.i.d. design, and not on the real coins. It beats both proved singles only under strong price noise and only for increases (Q17e). (2) *Is it never much worse than the best?* With equal weights it is 0% to 5% slower than the best frequency on persistent changes and needs 1.32 times the 5-minute delay on the trend; with balanced weights 11% to 18% and 1.17 times. Where one frequency fails badly (the one-minute detector on the trend, the coarse ones on short bursts), every average keeps most of the detection. (3) *Does it help under price noise?* Only partly. Every average that contains the one-minute detector inherits its invalidity. The proved 5 and 15 gap average helps for increases at γ = 2 and costs for decreases.

**Caveats.**

* The guarantee assumes each frequency's null holds with the true baselines. Real returns cluster in volatility (E9) and may violate the coarse nulls in ways the one-minute null does not (section 2.1 of the note). The plug-in baseline used on real data has no proof for any frequency (Q17b shows how much it adds). A confidence-sequence baseline for each frequency (`docs/theory_multifreq.md`, section 8) is sketched, not implemented.
* Under price noise, only the gap monitors without the one-minute detector are proved, and only for independent noise.
* The synthetic designs are i.i.d. apart from the noise and the trend. The trend is one kind of dependence, and other designs could favour other frequencies.
* Median delays are among detected paths only; when detection shares differ a lot (for example 15 minutes at ×0.8), compare the shares first.
* γ = 2 was added to the design after the γ = 1 results showed the one-minute detector still fastest (`docs/iterations/E17.md`); all other settings were fixed before any run, and the γ = 1 numbers did not change (same seeds).
* On real data, alarms across coins are correlated and the next-day windows of random times overlap, so the pooled intervals are optimistic (as in E6). The comparison between single frequencies is not at equal alarm rates.
* The references were checked through web-search listings only, because the full texts could not be opened from this machine: Vovk and Wang (2021), "E-values: calibration, combination and applications", Annals of Statistics 49(3), 1736 to 1754 (Project Euclid and Royal Holloway listings); Choe and Ramdas, "Combining evidence across filtrations", arXiv:2402.09698 (arXiv listing). We use only their basic points, as stated in the theory note.
