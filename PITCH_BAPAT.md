# Pitch for Prof. Sudeep Bapat

*A short brief for a first meeting, written as answers to ten questions. Every number has a label: [PUBLISHED] from your paper, [SYNTHETIC] from our simulations, [REAL] from our runs on market data. Details are in the [experiment report](docs/experiment_report.md); proofs are in [`docs/math.md`](docs/math.md) and [`docs/theory_delay.md`](docs/theory_delay.md).*

## 1. What problem are we solving?

We want an **online alarm for volatility changes**: a tool that reads one-minute returns as they arrive and says "volatility has gone up" or "volatility has gone down" soon after it happens, while **guaranteeing** how rarely it raises a false alarm, however long it runs and however often someone looks at it.

## 2. Why does it matter?

Risk limits, position sizes, margin requirements and model refits should all react when volatility changes regime. Two things make an online alarm hard. First, a few jumps or fat-tailed returns distort ordinary volatility measures, so classical detectors fire on jumps rather than on regime changes. Second, a test that is checked every minute will eventually fire by chance unless it is built for repeated looks. Existing tools solve one problem or the other, not both with a finite-sample guarantee.

## 3. What did the paper propose?

Bapat (2026) estimates integrated quarticity (the quantity behind confidence intervals for realized volatility) robustly: each return is clipped at c times its median absolute deviation (MAD), and the clipped fourth powers are smoothed with a kernel. Clipping bounds the influence of any single return, so jumps and outliers cannot dominate. The paper shows good performance in simulations with jumps and noise and on one-minute data for five cryptocurrencies.

## 4. What did we implement?

* Your estimators exactly as printed, normalised versions, and all the benchmark estimators of your Tables 3 and 4 (`src/avmonitor/robust.py`).
* A streaming monitor (`src/avmonitor/monitor.py`) that clips each squared return at c × MAD from past data only, X_t = min(r_t², τ²), and runs **betting e-detectors** on X_t, one for increases and one for decreases. An e-detector keeps a running "wealth" from bets against "nothing has changed"; it alarms when the wealth reaches A.
* Three ways to set the "normal" level: plug-in, plug-in with a margin, and a confidence sequence with a proof (`confseq.py`); forecast-relative monitoring for clustered volatility (`forecast.py`).
* Real-data pipelines for Binance BTC, ETH, XRP, SOL and DOGE (your five coins), Bitstamp BTC/USD and the Nifty 50 index, with a command line and an executed notebook. About 240 tests, and one command (`scripts/reproduce.py`) that checks the data hashes, runs the tests and reruns the experiments.

## 5. What did we independently validate?

* **Your tables.** With one fitted volatility, the printed formulas reproduce your simulation tables for the plain kernel, and the published crypto table is consistent with unnormalised kernel weights, the raw MAD and a sample with about 2,000 missing minutes (E1, E6) [PUBLISHED vs SYNTHETIC and REAL]. Once both kernels are put on the same scale, **your robust kernel beats the plain kernel in most simulation cells** (E1).
* **Our guarantee.** Proved: average time to a false alarm at least A, and P(false alarm within the first m steps after calibration) ≤ m / A (`docs/math.md`, Theorems A and B). Measured: 1.1% to 1.4% within the first day against a 5% bound for four very different return laws (E9) [SYNTHETIC]. On later days the proved bound is on the long-run average, 0.05 false alarms per day, unless the monitor restarts every day (TH6). The bound itself is not loose: an adversarial but legitimate return law reaches 95% of it for a single bet (E11, exact dynamic programming).
* **Robustness.** With jumps or fat tails, Gaussian likelihood-ratio and CUSUM detectors raise a false alarm within a day on 70% to 97% of paths; the robust e-detector stays at 0.3% to 2.3% and detects a 50% volatility rise in a median of 36 to 49 steps (E4) [SYNTHETIC].

## 6. What did our experiments demonstrate?

* **The clipping step of your estimator is what makes a model-free alarm possible.** No nonparametric test can detect a *fall* in the variance itself (a one-sided form of Bahadur and Savage 1956, `docs/math.md` 5.3), but it can detect a fall in a bounded quantity such as the clipped variance.
* **The price of robustness is small and explained.** On clean normal data the robust detector is 14%, 35% and 73% slower than a Gaussian likelihood-ratio detector for volatility rises of 25%, 50% and 100%. A ratio of information numbers predicts these penalties (1.16, 1.37, 1.95), and splits them into a truncation cost, a cost of making no distributional assumption (the largest part) and a cost of a finite set of bets (2% to 4%) (E11) [SYNTHETIC].
* **On real data the alarms mean something.** On your five coins, alarms are followed by volatility on the flagged side of EWMA and HAR forecasts 71% to 83% of the time against about 50% at random times, in and out of sample (E6). Around FOMC statements, the streaming monitor (the design fixed in advance) flags 8 to 9 of 16 within six hours, against 11% to 17% of control windows on the same weekday and clock time: every statement at which it was monitoring rather than recalibrating. A monitor calibrated on the day before each statement flags 14 to 15 of 16, against 29% to 32% of controls (E12). On Nifty 50, 9 of 11 Union Budget sessions raise an alarm against 25% of ordinary sessions, and 78% of increase alarms in a held-out period are followed by a more volatile session against 47% at random (E7) [REAL].
* **Against published methods.** Bayesian online change-point detection (BOCPD, Adams and MacKay 2007) raises false alarms on 53% to 73% of days with fat tails or jumps at a threshold that gives 1% on normal data; our monitor stays at 1.2% to 1.3% with the plug-in baseline (measured, no proved bound) and at 0.0% to 0.1% with the confidence-sequence baseline (proved 5% bound). On clean data BOCPD detects large decreases two to three times faster, and small decreases, or decreases in data with ticks or price noise, 1.0 to 1.9 times faster [SYNTHETIC]. On your five coins, with every method tuned to the same numbers of up and down alarms, our alarms are followed by a day beyond the forecast more often than those of competitors with windows of 2 to 4 hours (78.5% and 80.5% against 58.7% to 71.7%; in a held-out half-year 82.0% and 69.7% against 56.7% to 71.5%, where BOCPD on clipped returns is not significantly different on down alarms, 67.0% against 69.7%, p = 0.47). Given windows of half a day to a day, BOCPD on clipped returns and the adjacent-window test match us on down alarms (plain BOCPD stays lower), and the best competitor, picked after seeing the results, trails by about 6 points on up alarms, a borderline difference; on a four-hour horizon the short-window competitors do as well or better; on Nifty no first-run difference is significant (E13) [REAL]. The comparison with the volatility jump test of Bibinger, Jirak and Vetter (2017) uses our reconstruction of it, not yet checked against the paper's equations; its low power in our first runs came from its short windows.

## 7. What did we discover?

* **Clipping sets a speed limit for detecting decreases**: clipping caps the evidence that any valid bet can gather against a fall at log(B / (B - 1)), about 1 / c² nats per step. At c = 3 and A = 7800, even a total collapse of volatility takes any Shiryaev-Roberts e-detector built from valid bets at least 58 steps (64 at the two-sided level 2A), and our default bets at least 73 (79). A separate clip for the decrease side (c_down = 2, keeping c = 3 for increases) cut simulated median decrease delays by 13% to 51% for Gaussian, Student t4 and jump returns, and by 32% to 52% for prices rounded to ticks of 0.25 and 0.75 standard deviations, but was 12% slower for a 20% fall at a tick of 0.5 standard deviations; on five coins, which have few zero returns, it was neutral to slightly positive. Its false alarm guarantee holds with a known baseline or with the confidence-sequence baseline, which now supports it; the simulations used the plug-in baseline, whose one-day false alarm rates (5.0% to 6.7%) are empirical only (E11, E11f) [SYNTHETIC and REAL]. The limit is not an artefact of betting: no procedure with the same guarantee, randomised or not, can alarm on a complete collapse before step 45, and across 18 post-change laws the default grid takes 1.7 to 2.2 times the smallest mean delay any valid procedure could achieve (TH5) [proof and SYNTHETIC].
* **An estimated baseline quietly breaks the guarantee over long horizons**: 41% to 44% false alarms over five days, where a known baseline would guarantee at most 25% (E10, E14), and 51% to 57% over seven days, where it would guarantee at most 35% (ALT5). We proved that no bound of the form C m / A can hold for a plug-in baseline at a fixed calibration length, because a share of calibration windows that does not shrink as A grows gives some bet a positive drift under the null; that this share is positive is proved for the four simulated laws of E14, and its size (77% to 83% of windows at 390 steps) is a numerical estimate (E14, Proposition A.6 and Lemma A.7). A confidence-sequence baseline restores a proof, P(false alarm within m) ≤ δ + m / A (E8), and a rank-based detector needs no baseline at all (E14).
* **The blind period after an alarm can be removed with a proof.** A rank (conformal) e-detector restarted at once after each alarm keeps an exact m / A bound and catches 98% to 99.6% of changes in synthetic streams with about one change a day, against 56% to 66% for monitors that recalibrate for a day after each alarm (65% to 66% for the default plug-in), which are blind 57% to 71% of the time (E14) [SYNTHETIC].
* **Volatility clustering is itself a change in conditional variance.** Dividing by a fitted GARCH forecast brings false alarms back to the level of the true model, which restores the guarantee when the forecast model is right. But a forecast that keeps adapting also absorbs most of a real level shift, and we derived by how much in closed form: a doubling of the long-run variance raises the ratio of squared returns to the forecast by only 9% at persistence 0.99, and no shift, however large, can push it above (1 − β)/α = 1.2 (E10) [SYNTHETIC].
* **No adversary inside the theorem's conditions broke the bound.** We searched for null streams (conditional mean of the clipped squared return held at its baseline) that maximise false alarms. The worst, which watches the detector's state, always uses the widest legal spread and, when one draw can reach the threshold, lands exactly on it, reached 2.62% within the first day against 5% and 18.1% over five days against 25%. Outside the conditions every failure matched a broken condition and vanished when it was restored (for ticks, the same values drawn independently): clustering from a mean drift of 3.9% at one day (1.6% over five days), an estimated baseline (42% over five days), coarse price ticks (E16) [SYNTHETIC].
* **How fast the guarantee degrades is quantified.** If the conditional mean can drift above its baseline by at most δ, the bound m/A is multiplied by a closed-form factor: the one-day 5% becomes 10% at a drift of 1.63% and reaches 1 at about 3.7%. The plug-in baseline's estimation error is such a drift, with a proved one-day bound of 42% at 390 calibration steps, within 1.3 times the worst stream our adversarial search found (TH4) [proof and SYNTHETIC]. TH5 locates the boundary: the plug-in keeps its level when the horizon is short compared with the square root of the calibration length and fails beyond it, and a margin of Φ⁻¹(1 - δ / 2) standard errors restores m/A + δ [proof].
* **What "5% per day" means is now exact.** Theorem B bounds the first day after each (re)calibration and the long-run average of 0.05 false alarms per day, not every later day: on later days we measured 4.7% for i.i.d. returns, 5.25% for a stream that watches the detector and 28% after nine calm days. Restarting the statistics at every day boundary gives a proved 5% for every day at a cost of 2% to 14% in mean delay for 1440-step days (TH6) [proof and SYNTHETIC].
* **The blind period after an alarm costs real detections.** In four pre-registered landmark shocks, the streaming monitor flagged all three Nifty 50 shocks (11 to 172 minutes after the open) but none of the crypto ones within a day, because it had alarmed hours earlier as each episode began and was still recalibrating; given a fresh calibration it flagged all 18 cases in 15 to 207 minutes. In an exploratory run decided afterwards, the monitors of E14 with no blind period, at a similar alarm rate, flagged 15 and 17 of the 18 cases within a day (E18, case studies, not evidence) [REAL].
* **Combining time scales needs care.** The usual proof does not cover an average of detectors run on 1, 5 and 15 minute returns checked every minute, because a coarse detector is not a fair bet between its block ends; a union bound proves only three times the budget. Counting each coarse detector only at the wealth it is sure to keep gives a proved bound, Σ w_k ⌈m/k⌉ / A, when each frequency's null holds with known baselines. With independent simulated returns the combination is never faster than the best single frequency on a persistent change, but it catches 95% to 97% of paths after a jump in the autocorrelation of returns that leaves the one-minute variance unchanged and that the one-minute detector almost never sees (E17) [SYNTHETIC].
* **Price-level microstructure noise breaks the guarantee on raw returns** (false alarms rise from 5% to 41.5% per day as noise grows), and non-overlapping pre-averaged blocks restore it (ALT1) [SYNTHETIC].
* **On one-minute crypto data, large returns come in bursts**, not as isolated jumps: 44% to 65% have a large neighbour, against 8.9% by chance. Our normalised version of your robust kernel then comes out at 0.33 to 0.42 times MedRQ. The true quarticity is unknown on real data, but if a calibrated burst model is right, any estimator that clips against a scale measured over hours is biased down by about 35% to 59%, and a local scale removes most of that (ALT3) [REAL and SYNTHETIC].
* **The honest bottom line on real data**: alarms beat random times by 21 to 38 points in every comparison after adjustment for multiplicity and dependence between days (E20), but against a strong forecast (HAR plus the last hour's realised variance) increase alarms add no significant information and decrease alarms only a little, no more than a 240-minute ratio rule, with no out-of-sample loss gain. A simple ratio rule tuned with hindsight is about as precise as the e-detector (E6e; 1 of 48 comparisons favours the e-detector after adjustment). What the e-detector adds over that rule is a false-alarm guarantee with settings fixed in advance (proved with the confidence-sequence baseline; the plug-in baseline used on real data has only measured rates); the published change-point methods of E13, at the same alarm counts, are less precise on crypto with windows of 2 to 4 hours, but given day-long windows the clipped and adjacent-window versions match the e-detector on down alarms and the best of them trails it by a borderline 6 points on up alarms.

## 8. What are the limitations?

* The guarantee needs the clipped squared returns to keep their conditional mean while nothing changes. Clustering, price noise and estimated baselines violate this; each has a fix, and each fix costs speed.
* The bound is about four times conservative with the default set of bets for ordinary returns (E9, E11), and about two times at one day (1.4 times over five days) against the worst null stream an adversarial search found (E16).
* After an alarm the default monitor recalibrates and is blind about 70% of the time on real crypto data (E12). The awake monitors of E14 watch every release instead, but without a null band the rank version raises 3.6 to 4.3 alarms per crypto day.
* Our crypto and Nifty data come from public mirrors, not from the exchanges, and your paper does not name its data source.
* Full list: [`docs/limitations.md`](docs/limitations.md).

## 9. What should be done next?

1. A short joint paper on the monitor (a draft is in `paper/`): the bounded-statistic argument, the delay theory and the decrease speed limit, the confidence-sequence baseline, and the crypto and Nifty applications.
2. An estimation follow-up on your estimator: a local-scale plug-in version that reaches 95% efficiency in simulation (ALT3), which needs a central limit theorem.
3. A sequential-estimation bridge to your own field (ALT6, built): purely sequential fixed-accuracy estimation of the volatility level. Chow and Robbins on clipped squared returns, which runs on a running quarticity estimate, covers 94% to 96% at about the ideal sample size for every return law we tried, where raw squares fail under fat tails; an anytime-valid confidence sequence costs an exact factor 2 log(2/α) / z² = 1.92 more (1.55 with a running intersection); on BTC, about 50 minutes give plus or minus 20% on volatility. A natural next step is to use your robust kernel estimator as the variance plug-in and study its stopping time.
4. Finish the benchmark against published methods (E13): check our reconstruction of the Bibinger, Jirak and Vetter test against the paper's equations, and try BOCPD with a heavy-tailed likelihood, since BOCPD is faster than our monitor for decreases on clean data.
