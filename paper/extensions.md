This note collects four extensions of the monitor that the paper summarises in one paragraph each (Sections 6.1 and 6.5 of the paper): a running truncation level with restarts that leave no blind period after an alarm (Section 1), many assets with false discovery control (Section 2), several sampling frequencies at once (Section 3), and an adversarial search over null streams (Section 4). Section, theorem, table and supplement numbers refer to the paper unless they carry the prefix E, which marks the tables of this note. In Section 3, and only there, $k$ denotes a sampling frequency in minutes rather than a bet.

The proofs of the results stated here are in the theory notes of the accompanying repository (`docs/theory_running_tau.md`, `docs/theory_panel.md` and `docs/theory_multifreq.md`) and are not repeated. Labels in parentheses, such as (E14, Q14a), name the section of the repository's experiment report (`docs/experiment_report.md`) that holds the run and its result files; "simulated" and "market data" mark the source of each result. The market data are those of Section 7 of the paper.

## 1 A running truncation level and restarts

The monitor of the paper fixes $\tau$ before monitoring and recalibrates for a day after every alarm. Three constructions relax this, with proofs in the theory note on running truncation levels (E14). (A) A band $L(s) \le m(s) \le U(s)$ for the truncated mean $m(s) = \mathbb{E}[\min(r^2, s^2)]$ that holds for all truncation levels $s$ at once with probability $1 - \delta$; the increase side then bets against $U(\tau_t)$ and the decrease side against $L(\tau_t)$ for any predictable $\tau_t$, and $\mathbb{P}(\text{false alarm within } m) \le \delta + m/A$. The band is built from empirical Bernstein confidence sequences on a grid of 13 levels, or from a time-uniform bound on the distribution function. (B) Smoothed conformal p-values, the ranks of $\lvert r_t\rvert$ among all earlier absolute returns, which are i.i.d. uniform when the returns are exchangeable (Vovk, Nouretdinov and Gammerman 2003); betting on them gives $\mathbb{P}(\text{false alarm within } m) \le m/A$ exactly, with no baseline, no $\delta$ and no $\tau$. Its null, exchangeability, is stronger than the conditional-mean null, and it reacts to any change in the law of $\lvert r_t\rvert$, not only to a change of scale. Conformal test martingales for change-point detection were developed by Vovk et al. (2021); our rank version is of this kind. (C) Restarts with no blind period, which bet from the first return after an alarm. The band version learns its band from the returns after the restart while monitoring; the Shiryaev-Roberts chain started at step $j$ bets against the band as it stood before $j$, multiplied by the indicator that the band has not failed by then, an event known when the chain starts, which keeps each chain a supermartingale. The rank version restarts its reference set empty and bets from the first return after the alarm. Its bound $m/A$ then holds for the new segment if the returns of the segment are exchangeable conditionally on the returns up to the alarm; the condition is stated given the past because the alarm time depends on the data. It holds for i.i.d. returns and for any infinite exchangeable sequence (Remark B.3 of the theory note).

**Results on simulated data.** We used 5000 null paths per cell, the four laws of Table 3 of the paper (Gaussian, Student $t_3$, jumps and price ticks), and alarm levels for which every proved bound is 5% per 390-step day. Within one day the plug-in stayed below 5% (4.0% to 4.9% with 390 calibration steps), but within five days it alarmed on 40.6% to 42.9% of null paths, where a known baseline would guarantee at most 25%, with or without a running $\tau$. The paragraph after this one gives the explanation. The rank detectors, with likelihood-ratio bets in rank space or with indicator bets, were valid at their nominal level and used their budget (1.7% to 3.2% per day, 14% to 18% over five days, against 0.8% to 1.7% and 12% to 14% for the oracle with the true baseline), while the band of route A and the confidence sequence of Section 4.2 of the paper were valid with a very wide margin (at most 0.26% over five days). In delay, the rank detector with likelihood-ratio bets beat the plug-in on every decrease, by a factor of two to three for the larger ones (sd ratio 0.5: 25 to 34 steps against 69 to 77), partly because its bets are clipped likelihood ratios for a normal scale change, while the bets on $X$ are linear with $\lambda$ at most 0.5; it was slower on increases under Student $t_3$ returns (sd ratio 1.25: 243 to 260 steps against 182 to 194). Route A with 1440 calibration steps was close to the confidence sequence on increases and faster on decreases with a running $\tau$ (Gaussian, sd ratio 0.5: 71 steps against 105), but with 390 calibration steps it missed most small changes. On small increases the running $\tau$ costs a little for Gaussian returns (1440 calibration steps, sd ratio 1.25: 336 against 326 steps with $\tau$ fixed) and more under Student $t_3$ tails (937 against 783 steps, and 76% against 88% of changes detected), because after an increase $\tau_t$ moves up to a level where the band is wider (E14, Q14a and Q14b).

**The plug-in at a fixed calibration length.** The theory note on running truncation levels shows why the plug-in fails, at a fixed calibration length $n$. Let $T_A$ be the first alarm of the two-sided plug-in rule, and for $\eta > 0$ and $\kappa < \infty$ let $p_{\eta,\kappa}$ be the probability that the calibration window gives the best bet of the detector, on either side, an expected log growth of at least $\eta$ per monitored step under the null, with a largest factor of at most $e^{\kappa}$; $p_{\eta,\kappa}$ depends on $n$ and on the law of the returns but not on $A$. Proposition A.6 of the note shows that $\liminf_{A \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(T_A \le m_A) \ge p_{\eta,\kappa}$ for $m_A = \lceil (\log A)^2 \rceil$, and Lemma A.7 proves $p_{\eta,\kappa} > 0$ for some $\eta$ and $\kappa$ under all four laws of E14 (Gaussian, Student $t_3$, jumps and price ticks). Since $m_A/A \to 0$, no bound $\mathbb{P}(T_A \le m) \le C\,m/A$ with a constant $C$ holds for all $A$ and $m$. The lemma uses calibration windows that are far calmer than the law they come from, and therefore extremely rare, so it says nothing about how often a positive drift occurs; the share of calibration windows with a positive drift is a Monte Carlo estimate from 2000 calibration windows per law, 77% to 83% at 390 calibration steps and 56% to 68% at 1440, with standard errors below one percentage point (E14, Q14w). Nor does the proposition account for the size of the failure at $A = 7800$: its own lower bound on the five-day rate is 0.5% to 0.8% at 390 calibration steps, against the 40.6% to 42.9% simulated (E14, Q14a). Corollary 10 of the paper gives a computable bound for the plug-in at any fixed calibration length, which is not of the form $C\,m/A$.

**Restarts without a blind period (simulated).** After a restart with no change (4000 paths per cell, the four laws above), the rank version alarmed within 390 steps on 2.70% to 3.35% of paths and within 1950 steps on 15.7% to 17.0% (bounds 5% and 25%), and the band version on at most 0.03% (bounds 5% and 21%). With a change 120 steps after the restart, the rank version detected it in a median of 20 to 70 steps for Gaussian returns and 42 to 115 steps for Student $t_3$ returns, on 65% to 99% of paths; the standard protocol, which calibrates on the first 1440 returns of the segment, found at most 61% of these changes within 2000 steps, 1660 to 1830 steps after the change. Even a change 30 steps after the restart was often found (halving of the standard deviation: 91% of Gaussian and 70% of Student $t_3$ paths). The band version detected nothing when the change came 30 steps after the restart, because its band from so few returns is very wide and later snapshots already contain post-change returns, and at most 34% of changes other than a halving when it came after 120 steps; where both detected, the rank version was about 3 to 10 times faster (E14, Q14f).

**Restarts on market data.** On the five coins (July to December 2025, calibration 1440 minutes, band 0.25) and the Nifty 50 (2024 and 2025), the standard protocol monitored only 30% to 33% of crypto minutes (40% for the Nifty 50), while the restarted monitors watched all of them and raised 0.66 to 0.88 alarms per crypto day (0.23 to 0.25 per Nifty session), about as many as the standard protocol (0.66 to 0.70). The band 0.25 has two meanings here. For the monitors that bet on $X$, the band version included, it widens the baseline by 25% in each direction; for the rank version it divides each payoff by 1.25, which tolerates a conditional mean payoff of up to 1.25 and has no direct meaning in units of volatility. The setting 0.25 of the event study of Section 7.3 of the paper is used for both (E14, Q14c). Pooled over the six series, 80% (band version) and 74% (rank version) of their increase alarms and 85% and 90% of their decrease alarms were followed by a next day on the flagged side of the baseline, against 82% and 90% for the standard protocol. Without a band the rank version alarmed 3.6 to 4.3 times per crypto day with lower confirmation rates (67% and 73%): right after a restart its reference set is small, and it soon rejects exchangeability, which clustered volatility violates. Among the monitors that recalibrate, the proved ones alarmed less than the plug-in and were confirmed about as often after increase alarms (78% to 83% against 82%) and more often after decrease alarms (94% to 97% against 90%) (E14, Q14d).

**Month-long streams (simulated).** In 40 streams of 30 days of 1440 steps per cell, with every monitor restarting after each alarm at an alarm level of 5% per 1440-step day and band 0: with no change, the plug-in raised 0.094 (Gaussian) and 0.084 (Student $t_3$) false alarms per day against a nominal 0.05, while the proved monitors stayed within their per-day bounds (rank restart 0.029 and 0.035 against 0.050; band restart and confidence sequence essentially 0). With the standard deviation multiplied by 2, 1.5, 0.67 or 0.5 about once a day, the monitors that recalibrate for a day after each alarm were blind 57% to 71% of the time, awake at 31% to 49% of the changes, and detected 56% to 66% of them within a day (65% to 66% for the default plug-in; median delay 78 to 166 steps); the rank restart detected 99.6% and 98.0% (median 34 and 60 steps) and the band restart 94.3% and 77.8% (median 97 and 153) (E14, Q14h).

**Event study rerun (market data; exploratory, outside the pre-specified family).** With the design of Section 7.3 of the paper unchanged except for the monitor, the restarted monitors were watching at all 114 releases (48 FOMC statements and 66 CPI releases over three coins), against 55 for the streaming plug-in. Up alarms within six hours rose from 26 of 48 FOMC statements (plug-in) to 30 (band restart) and 35 (rank restart), and from 21 of 66 CPI releases to 24 and 31; every awake cell had a nominal stratified $p \le 0.006$ against matched controls, and the BTC CPI comparison gave nominal $p = 0.003$ and $0.0003$ (0.11 for the plug-in in the primary analysis). A different monitor, run after the primary test, cannot rescue that test; these numbers describe the restarted monitors only. The rank restart paid with a higher control rate (13% to 19% of matched control windows, against 7% to 14% for the band restart and 8% to 17% for the plug-in), so part of its gain is more alarms everywhere. Being awake did not mean flagging every release, and compared at the same releases the restarted monitors flagged fewer. At the 26 FOMC statements at which the plug-in was monitoring, it flagged all 26, the band restart 19 and the rank restart 23; at the 29 CPI releases at which it was monitoring, it flagged 19 (its 21 above include two releases at which it woke up within the six hours), the band restart 10 and the rank restart 14. A monitor that restarted shortly before a release compares the release against a short or volatile baseline, which may explain part of the gap; we did not test this (E14, Q14g).

**Cost (simulated).** On a shared machine, in plain Python with NumPy, one return at a time: 13 microseconds per return for the plug-in, 15 to 16 for the running band, 44 for the rank monitor (whose sorted reference set grows when there is no alarm) and 47 for the band restart (77 in its learning phase); only the ratios are informative (E14, Q14e). On the real series above, measured later while the machine was more heavily loaded, the band restart cost 97 to 182 microseconds per return, because it restarts its learning phase after every alarm, the rank restart 45 to 70 and the monitors that recalibrate 5.8 to 45.6 (E14, Q14d). Every monitor costs well under a millisecond per return.

## 2 Many assets: false discovery control

A user rarely watches one asset. With $K$ monitors at level $\alpha$ per day each, the chance of some false alarm on a quiet day grows to about $K\alpha$, and Bonferroni's correction slows every monitor. The e-BH procedure (Wang and Ramdas 2022) controls the false discovery rate for e-values under any dependence, but a Shiryaev-Roberts statistic is not an e-value at a data-dependent time. E15 therefore gives each asset a mixture e-process for "no change since the block started", $E_n = \sum_{j \le n} w_j L_n^{(j)} + \sum_{j > n} w_j$ averaged over bets and sides (weights $w_j$ over start times, fixed in advance, summing to 1), applies e-BH to these e-processes at every step, and stops each asset at its discovery ("sticky e-BH"). Within one block and whatever the dependence between assets, the false discovery rate is at most $\alpha$ at every stopping time, the expected largest false discovery proportion over the block is at most $\alpha(1 + \log(1/\alpha))$, about 20% for $\alpha = 5\%$, and with no change anywhere the probability of any discovery is at most $\alpha$ (theory note on panel monitoring, Theorem 1). The proof needs each asset's null to hold given the past of the whole panel, which is stronger than its own-past null when volatility spills over between assets with a lag. Dandapanthula and Ramdas (2025) study multiple testing across many streams in sequential change detection; we have not compared the two approaches.

**Simulated data.** In simulation (true baselines, blocks of 390 steps, $\alpha = 5\%$, independent assets or a common factor with pairwise correlation 0.7 and common jumps), the naive rule raised a false alarm in 37.2% of quiet blocks with 50 independent assets, while e-BH and Bonferroni stayed at 0.3% to 0.9% for $K$ from 5 to 50. With changes, the false discovery rate of e-BH stayed below 1% in all 48 designs, and e-BH found on average 5.0 percentage points more changed assets than Bonferroni (at most 21.3 points more), but its probability of at least one false discovery, which it does not control, reached 8.0% at $K = 50$. Two negative results: with the confidence-sequence baseline the power for a 25% increase fell from about 93% to 36% to 41%, much more than the multiplicity correction costs; and a guarantee per daily block is not a guarantee for a horizon of ten blocks, over which daily e-BH had a 23.5% chance of at least one false discovery against 4.25% for daily Bonferroni (E15).

**Market data.** The panel monitor was run on the five coins with one block per UTC day, $\alpha = 5\%$ per day, the plug-in baseline of the previous 1440 returns and band 0.25 or 0.5, and on the five coins together with the Nifty 50 index, each asset on its own clock (E15). Three findings stand out. First, the strict daily null is false on most coin-days: at band 0.25 e-BH discovered 86% to 87% of coin-days, so the false discovery guarantee is nearly empty in practice: under persistent clustering the null is false on most days (Sections 6.4 and 7.4 of the paper). Second, the multiplicity rule changed the timing more than the set: e-BH found only 3.7% to 8.7% more coin-days than Bonferroni, but on the coin-days found by both it flagged first 71% to 77% of the time (median 2 to 4 minutes earlier). Third, discoveries pointed the right way: an increase discovery was followed by a higher robust volatility over the next day 78% to 83% of the time against 46% to 50% at matched random times, and a decrease discovery by a lower one 85% to 97% of the time against 49% to 55%. As with the ratio rule of Section 7.2 of the paper, a per-asset threshold chosen with hindsight to make as many discoveries as e-BH had them confirmed at about the same rate: in the 12 cells (panel, period, band and direction) the two confirmation rates differed by at most 1.4 percentage points, with two-proportion $p$-values from 0.56 to 1.00; the two sets of discoveries overlap, so these tests are indicative only. The e-BH level is fixed in advance and carries a guarantee when the baselines are known. With the Nifty 50 in the panel, its discoveries were confirmed 87% to 95% of the time, and four times (band 0.25) e-BH discovered it after its session had closed, because crypto discoveries later in the day lowered the e-BH bar below its frozen e-value.

## 3 Several sampling frequencies

**Set-up.** Frequency $k \in \lbrace 1, 5, 15 \rbrace$ (minutes) sums the one-minute returns of non-overlapping $k$-minute blocks or, in the *gap* version, the last $k - 1$ returns of each block, so that consecutive blocks share no price. Each block return $R_b^{(k)}$ gives $X_b^{(k)} = \min((R_b^{(k)})^2, \tau_k^2)$, with $\tau_k$ three scaled MADs of $k$-minute returns, and frequency $k$ runs its own increase and decrease detectors over blocks with the default grids; between block ends its statistic $M^{(k)}$ keeps its last value. The weights $w_k \ge 0$ sum to 1. Each frequency needs its own null, $\mathbb{E}[X_b^{(k)} \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \le m_k^{\uparrow}$ for every block (and $\ge m_k^{\downarrow}$ on the decrease side), where $s$ is the minute before the block starts and $\mathcal{F}_s$ holds all one-minute information up to $s$. The one-minute null does not imply it in general: if every return of a block takes a fair random sign drawn for that block, $r_t = S_b \lvert e_t \rvert$ with $e_t$ i.i.d. standard normal, the one-minute null holds exactly but a 5-minute block has mean square 17.7 instead of 5. It does hold with the true baselines for i.i.d. returns with any tails or jumps, and for gap blocks under i.i.d. price noise on an efficient price with independent increments. The statements below are proved in the theory note on multi-frequency monitoring; we keep its numbering. Combining e-processes built in different filtrations is studied in general by Choe and Ramdas (2024).

**Why the usual proof fails (Proposition 2 of the note).** An average of e-values is an e-value (Vovk and Wang 2021), but a $k$-minute detector is an e-detector on its own clock, not on the one-minute clock: with $k = 2$, i.i.d. Gaussian returns, no truncation and one increase bet $\lambda$, a stopping time on the one-minute clock gives $\mathbb{E}[M_\tau] = 1 + p + 0.484\lambda$, while the expected number of completed blocks is only $1 + p$, with $p = \mathbb{P}(\lvert Z \rvert > 1) = 0.317$. This breaks the proof of Theorems 3 and 4 of the paper for the plain average checked every minute; it does not show that the average exceeds the bound.

**Certified average (Theorem 1 and Corollary 4 of the note).** Between block ends, bet $j$ of frequency $k$ is credited with $C_t = \min(M_s, \ell_j (M_s + 1))$, where $M_s$ is its value at the last block end and $\ell_j$ its smallest possible factor ($1 - \lambda_j$ for an increase bet, $1 - \varphi_j$ for a decrease bet); at block ends $C_t = M_t$. Each side averages $C_t$ over its grid and over frequencies with weights $w_k$, and the monitor alarms when either side reaches $2A$. If every frequency with positive weight satisfies both nulls with the baselines that the monitor uses, then for every $m \ge 1$
$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{alarm within } m \text{ minutes}) \le \frac{1}{A}\sum_k w_k \lceil m/k \rceil,
$$
and the mean time to the first alarm is at least $(A - o_w)/q_w$ minutes, with $q_w = \sum_k w_k/k$ and $o_w = \sum_k w_k (1 - 1/k)$. With recalibration after every alarm, the expected number of alarms within $m$ monitored minutes is at most $(m q_w + o_w)/(A - o_w)$. *Sketch.* $\mathbb{E}[M_{e(t)} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$, with $e(t)$ the end of the current block, is at least $C_t$ and grows in mean by at most 1 per block started, so Ville's inequality applies.

**Plain average and weighted maximum (Theorem 3 of the note).** With frozen values checked every minute and $N_k = \lfloor m/k \rfloor$, a union bound gives $\sum_k w_k N_k / A$ for the rule $\max_k w_k M^{(k)} \ge 2A$ and $(\sum_k \sqrt{w_k N_k})^2/A$ for the plain average, three times the certified bound when $w_k \propto k$ ("balanced" weights). Neither has a restart result.

**The price of not knowing the frequency (Proposition 3 of the note).** If only frequency $j$ sees a change, the certified average alarms when $M^{(j)}$ reaches $h_j = \sum_k w_k \lceil m/k \rceil / (w_j \lceil m/j \rceil)$ times the threshold that $j$ would need alone at the same budget. For any weights $\max_j h_j \ge 3$, the cost of a Bonferroni split, with equality for balanced weights; equal weights give 1.27, 6.33 and 19. For 5% per 1440-minute day, $A$ is 28,800, 5760 and 1920 for one frequency alone, 12,160 for equal and 4114 for balanced weights (E17, Q17a).

**Results on simulated data.** With the true baselines and i.i.d. Gaussian, Student $t_3$ or jump returns, every monitor alarmed on 0.32% to 2.62% of days, and the plain every-minute average stayed within 0.07 points of the certified one: the factor 3 is a gap in the proof, not in behaviour. With plug-in baselines the one-minute detector alarmed on 5.47% of Gaussian days, significantly above 5%, and the balanced average on 3.66% to 4.54% (Q17b). Under price noise ($r_t = e_t + u_t - u_{t-1}$, $u_t \sim N(0, \gamma^2)$ in units of the one-minute efficient standard deviation, as in the price-noise experiment of Section 6.4 of the paper) with true baselines, the one-minute detector alarmed on 6.88% and 12.06% of days at $\gamma = 1$ and 2, the balanced average on 3.09% and 6.50%, and the proved gap average of 5 and 15 minutes on 0.54% and 0.52%. At $\gamma = 2$ it detected persistent increases of 25% and 50% more often than either proved single gap detector (28.1% against 21.4% and 25.3%; 93.3% against 85.5% and 82.8%) but halvings less often than the 5-minute gap detector (47.2% against 56.5%); at the planned $\gamma = 1$ it did not help for increases (78.7% against 79.2% at sd ×1.25), and at neither level for decreases or a tripling lasting 60 minutes. The level $\gamma = 2$ was added after the $\gamma = 1$ results had been seen (Q17e).

**Results on market data.** On the five coins (July to December 2025, deseasonalised as in Section 7 of the paper, band 0.25, plug-in baselines, recalibration after every alarm), the one-minute detector made up 98.4% to 99.7% of the combined statistic at alarms on average. The averages alarmed 0.65 to 0.68 times per day (one-minute monitor 0.68), and their up and down alarms were followed by next-day volatility on the flagged side of the baseline 78% to 80% and 84% to 88% of the time, against 80% and 88%. The 5- and 15-minute detectors alone alarmed 0.43 and 0.26 times per day and were confirmed more often (up 90% and 95%, down 95% and 96%) (Q17f). In a comparison added after an independent review, the one-minute monitor with its two levels set per coin to give about as many up and down alarms as the 5-minute detector had its up alarms confirmed 90.5% of the time against 90.0% ($p = 0.88$) and its down alarms 96.4% against 94.6% ($p = 0.43$); matched to the 15-minute detector, its down alarms were followed by volatility below an EWMA forecast 88.9% of the time against 72.4% ($p = 0.0014$). The p-values treat alarms as independent and are optimistic (Q17g).

## 4 Adversarial null streams

The paper's bound $m/A$ holds for every stream in the class of Theorem 4, including streams that react to the detector's own state. This section asks how close to the bound such a stream can get, and what happens outside the class (E16).

**Set-up.** The detector is the full default: $c = 3$, six increase bets from 0.02 to 0.5, five decrease bets from 0.1 to 0.95 of $1/(B-1)$ with $B = \tau^2/m_0$, Shiryaev-Roberts statistics and each side alarming at $2A$, $A = 7800$, so the bound is 5% for a 390-step day and 25% for five days. Inside the class of Theorem 4 only the law of $Y_t = X_t/m_0$ matters: $Y_t \in [0, B]$ with conditional mean exactly 1. At each step both statistics are affine in $Y_t$, so a two-point law with mean 1 is as good as any law for the adversary (as in the proof of Lemma 15(a)), whose whole choice is a pair of points at each step, possibly depending on everything seen so far, including the detector's state. Any $B > 1$ is legitimate for a level fixed in advance (Gaussian returns give $B = 9.045$). The families searched were i.i.d. two- and three-point laws for $Y$; return laws with their own $\tau$ and $m_0$ (scale mixtures of two normals, Student $t$ with $\nu \ge 2.1$, returns rounded to a tick); exogenous switching between the shape $\lbrace 0, B \rbrace$ and the Gaussian shape; and adaptive laws: bold play, an 8-parameter policy family that contains it, and bold play after holding $Y_t = 1$ for a while. Search maxima are biased upwards, so the worst law of each family was re-run on fresh seeds (200,000 paths at one day, 60,000 at five days).

No interval reaches the bound. Against the worst stream found, the bound is about two times conservative at one day and 1.4 times over five days. Seeing the detector's state adds about half a point at one day and three points over five days (bold play against the best i.i.d. two-point law at the same $B$). The 8-parameter search beat bold play only on its own training paths (2.82% against 2.63%). Bold play uses 0.22 of the bound at a quarter of a day, 0.71 to 0.72 between about 2,000 and 4,000 steps and 0.62 at $m = A$; for $B$ from 1.2 to 39 its one-day rate stays between 2.0% and 2.6%, and at $B$ = 100 to 1,000 it is below its rate at $B = 2$ (Q16b).

**Where the slack sits, and what is proved.** In the optional stopping identity of Supplement S5, bold play has a stopping factor of 0.99, $F_2 = 0.49$ and $F_3 = 1.00$, against 1.00, 0.25 and 0.79 for Gaussian data: it removes the overshoot and doubles the share of expected wealth on alarmed paths. Under every law tried, the rest is wealth on paths that never alarm, 60% of it in the decrease bets and 36% in the increase bets 0.02, 0.05 and 0.1; whether a null law can move it onto alarmed paths is open. For single bets bold play comes within 0.03 of the bound fraction reached by the dynamic programme of E11 for the bets 0.5 and 0.2 and the largest decrease bet at one to ten days, and beats it for the bet 0.05 (0.26 against 0.18 at one day), whose programme value is a loose lower bound from a coarse grid of wealth states (0.232 on a finer grid) (Q16c). The only proved limit is a floor. On a day without an alarm every increase bet is at least where draws of 0 at every step would leave it and every decrease bet at least where draws of $B$ would, so the two-sided statistic is at least $c_{\min} = 8.3$; optional stopping gives $A\,P + c_{\min}\,(1 - P) \le m$ for the probability $P$ of a false alarm within $m$ steps, so $P \le (m - c_{\min})/(A - c_{\min}) = 4.90\%$ at one day, 0.98 of the bound.

**Outside the class.** *GARCH(1,1)* with Gaussian innovations and the true unconditional $\tau$ and $m_0$, on 64 cells (persistence 0.5 to 0.999, $\alpha$ from 0.002 to 0.3; 6000 paths of five days each): the rate was significantly above the bound in 43 cells at one day, in every cell whose mean absolute drift $\mathbb{E}\lvert \mu_t/m_0 - 1 \rvert$ of the conditional mean $\mu_t$ exceeded 5.6% and in none below 3.9% (over five days the cut lies between 1.6% and 2.5%), with a Spearman correlation of 0.99 between drift and rate. Betting against the true $\mu_t$ on the same paths, the detector stayed at or below 0.24 of the bound at one day and 0.56 over five days (Q16d). *A plug-in baseline* with i.i.d. returns and 390 calibration steps alarmed within five days on 41% to 42% of fresh paths under the worst law of each of four families, against 25%, while the same $\hat\tau$ with the true mean at that level stayed at or below 0.59 of the bound: the excess comes from $\hat m$. After a Gaussian calibration, replacing every monitored $X$ by the constant $m(\hat\tau)$, which keeps the conditional mean, raised the plug-in rate to 32.5% within one day, while that oracle never alarmed (Q16e). *Price-level ticks* (a Gaussian random walk rounded to a tick, true unconditional $\tau$ and $m_0$) exceeded the bound from a tick of 4 standard deviations over five days (28.8%, interval 28.0% to 29.7%), where the price is unchanged in 80% of steps and the conditional mean after an unchanged price is 0.88 of $m_0$, and from 6 at one day (17.6%). The same values drawn independently, with the same $\tau$ and $m_0$, stayed at or below 0.32 of the bound at one day and 0.57 over five days at every tick (at 4 standard deviations, 0.90% and 7.4%) (Q16f). A search finds lower bounds on the worst case only, the policy families are broad but not exhaustive, and everything here is simulated. Theorem 7 of the paper bounds the effect of a drift of the conditional mean such as the GARCH drift above, and Corollary 10 the effect of the plug-in.
